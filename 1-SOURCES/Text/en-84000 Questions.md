# 84000 Questions ^0

Chapter 1: Getting to know Buddhism ^1

1. Why am I here? How did I become who I am? Why do I like or dislike some people and things and not care about others? Why am I Chinese and not Arab, and how did I choose to be born in Beijing and not Morocco? Why do I even have a mind? In short, how does Buddhism explain why things are the way they are? ^2

According to Buddhism, we are here in this form and things are the way they are because of billions of causes and conditions, each of which has its own billions of causes and conditions. So even though we might be born into the same family and environment, there are billions of different causes and conditions that make some of us afraid of spiders while we have no qualms about stuffing lobster legs into our mouths, and that make some of us faint at the mere sight of blood while we love raw fish. ^3

But there is one cause that I have to single out, and that is our mind. That is the biggest cause. If there were no mind, there would be no being to experience the burden or the joy of all the other causes. ^4

2. Some people believe in an almighty force and are therefore suspicious about the Buddhist concept of karma, cause and condition. What do you think about that? ^5

For people who believe in an almighty force, the almighty itself is a cause and condition, though ultimately it may be the only cause and condition in which they believe. Of course I don’t think that relatively(on the level of relative truth) they are saying that when they cook an egg, there is a God or almighty force behind cooking this egg. If they believed that, they would just pray to God that the egg will be cooked, and not add water, switch on the gas, or create any of the other causes and conditions needed to cook the egg. ^6

Different people will anyhow believe in different things, and they have valid reasons. Different people will also have suspicions about others’ belief, and they have their valid reasons. For the Buddhists to believe that an almighty force is the cause of everything is very suspicious. Buddhists will not want to think that the almighty pulls all the strings, because Buddhists will think if the almighty has that power, then the almighty also has compassion. Why does he want us to suffer? Isn't he compassionate? Since he has the power, he should solve all the problems, and should not be enjoying us suffering. So Buddhist would rather like to believe that it is not one almighty ultimate cause that is causing all these, rather things appear and things disappear due to infinite causes and conditions. ^7

For a Buddhist, the creator of the almighty is one’s own mind ⎯ this mind that I mentioned above. It’s because our mind believes in an almighty creating us that we think there is a God. So actually we created this God and then we give credit to the God for creating us. ^8

3. People with no religious belief or who are very materialistic also don’t believe in the Buddhist concept of karma, cause and condition. What do you think about that? ^9

It is not that people who have no religious belief or are materialistic don’t believe in causes and conditions. It is just that their understanding of causes and conditions is limited, and so they only believe in the causes and conditions within their reach or that are right in front of them. What they can’t accept is all the causes and conditions that our small minds cannot fathom, imagine and reach. In other words, they may understand how someone with a traumatic family life in childhood may grow up depressed. But they don’t even think to ask how that person chose to be born into such a family in the first place. ^10

Another example is that a parent can send their child to the best school or university so they get the best job. They assume that they have done everything possible to create the right circumstances, causes and conditions. But the child may not get the job or be diagnosed with pancreatic cancer the day the job starts, which they might think very unfair. Then they might suffer, blame someone or get angry, even though they have no idea what they are getting angry at, which shows they have forgotten the hidden causes and conditions possibly gathered in past lives. ^11

So only understanding causes and conditions partially but not completely makes people fall into all sorts of hopes and fears that will lead them to miseries and sorrows. ^12

I have an Australian friend who is a very stanch lesbian and feminist with a strong character and dynamic thinking. Once she came to visit me in Bhutan, where I have an attendant who is a shy young Bhutanese boy who keeps to himself, is never outgoing and is certainly not a lady’s man. ^13

I was travelling at that time, and when I returned to Bhutan, I found my attendant missing because he and this lesbian woman had become lovers. A few months later, she even bore him a child. And gradually her hair style, perfume, and way of dressing changed from being elegantly boyish to being very feminine. ^14

So we never know what causes and conditions await us. There are so many surprises in the next corner, and causes and conditions are unpredictable to conventional minds. ^15

4. Could Rinpoche please explain interdependent arising[2] and also the Buddhist concept of emptiness? ^16

Believe it or not, understanding causes, conditions, and effects thoroughly and completely is the understanding of emptiness. Because when you understand causes and conditions completely, you will realize that there is not an independent or almighty original cause. When three or four sticks are put together with a wooden plank on top, if you then put a mug on it, that thing has suddenly become a table. If you put your butt on it, it becomes a chair and the table just as suddenly disappears. ^17

So a table or chair has been put together temporarily, but ⎯ beyond such temporary transitory collections ⎯ there is no original table or chair. ^18

Those who believe in an almighty believe that everything is dependent on someone else’s control and is not under your control. Those who believe only in materialism believe everything is under your own control. Both these misunderstandings can lead to suffering. ^19

But then causes and conditions are not that easy to understand, so there is always a reluctance to apprehend causes and conditions at the deeper level. Because when you really dig deeper and deeper into causes and conditions, the fact that there is no truly existing ultimate cause and condition becomes evident. This cognition will then bring us closer to the Buddhist concept of emptiness. ^20

I suggest seeing a Japanese film called Roshomon by Akira Kurosawa that tells a story from different people’s point of view with different causes and conditions, so there is not one reality that is the truth. That is basically how things are with us. In this way, interdependent arising and emptiness are synonymous. ^21

5. How is cosmic origin explained in Buddhism? ^22

For the sake of casual conversation, conventionally we can talk about origin, and we can also talk about the final end. But the moment we add the word cosmic, original or ultimate ⎯ in other words, the moment we want to know about the ultimate origin or ultimate end ⎯ then in Buddhism, since time is relative, there is no such thing as an ultimate beginning or ending. ^23

This kind of dialogue becomes very complicated because ⎯ from the world of speakable, listenable, thinkable and describable things ⎯ you are attempting to speak the unspeakable, hear the unlistenable, think the unthinkable, and describe the indescribable. So, the moment you try to talk or begin to think about something that cannot be spoken or thought about, there is always going to be a problem. ^24

Also, as soon as we talk about something unspeakable or indescribable, it already creates a sense of mystery. But actually there is no mystery. It is simply a raw truth, but because we human beings cannot see the truth directly, the only way we can try to understand the raw truth is through labelling. This paradox is well indicated in the great Taoist book Taode Jing, which says that “aname that can be given is not a name”, which shows that we human beings love to give names. ^25

In Buddhism too we have labelled this simplicity, this raw truth, as dharmakaya[3]. You can think dharmakaya is mythical and mystical, but the fact of matter is that dharmakaya is a simple truth. ^26

Because mystery has the connotation of being difficult or impossible to understand or explain, dharmakaya is probably seen as mythical. But the fact of the matter is that dharmakaya is even closer than in front of your nose. And that's about all I can say. People who ask what is the origin of everything have to keep in mind that there is a territory of relative truth and a territory of ultimate truth[4]. They can't sit inside the relative and expect an ultimate answer, or vice versa. ^27

6. Can a materialist approach to causes and conditions mislead us? ^28

It’s not that the vast majority of Chinese don’t believe in causes and conditions at all, but they only believe in materialistic causes and conditions that are evident to their senses. ^29

So they believe that if you have a lot of money or a good husband or get a promotion to the Politburo, you’ll be happy. But that is not always the case. Many people with money don’t have time to see the yellowness of a marigold; a good husband might not get enough erections; and a powerful Politburo member may have a skeleton in his closet that will come back to haunt him. ^30

So they have to realize that this materialistic game can never really uproot insecurity. This maybe a little difficult for people to understand, because somehow we have this blind notion that whoever has money can do or get whatever they want. Superficially that might temporarily seem true, but when you surrender too much to such material temptations, then your confidence is built on very uncertain things that can change. ^31

One of my students was educated in a very refined school in England. She’s very beautiful and many boys like her and try to impress her. One time when she came to see me, a wealthy Rinpoche’s son fell head over heels in love with her and tried so hard to court her by driving flashy cars, waving Louis Vuitton bags, and switching his Rolex watch to a Patek Philippe. But those things actually made her cringe because they’re the complete opposite of what she likes. She would much rather date someone who reads Shakespeare, listens to Wagner, and can talk about the intricate things in our lives. This clearly shows that if you become too materialistic, you will always have an inferiority complex. ^32

I have travelled several times to China, and while I am awed by the timeless and fascinating grandeur, depth and vastness of Chinese culture, tradition, philosophy, medicine and martial arts, I also can’t help thinking that modern Chinese may actually suffer from a serious inferiority complex so long as they base their feelings of superiority on the past. After all, the jeans that the Chinese wear, the gum they chew, the sandwiches they munch, and the rock and roll and pop songs to which they now listen have nothing originally Chinese about them. ^33

Likewise, the glittering shopping malls, the goods they sell, and even the models are all western in style, as if to say that Chinese boys and girls are not good enough to model clothes their own way. In fact I now see Chinese girls and boys trying their best to look just like western girls and boys, even to the extent of dieting and having plastic surgery. Such imitation may not develop a genuine confidence among the Chinese in the years to come. ^34

I might be wrong, but I don’t think there’s a single Chinese pop singer with any real influence outside China. I mean you never hear Chinese pop songs being widely sung in New York City, whereas everybody in China is wearing jeans, eating Kentucky Fried Chicken and trying to copy American materialism. I’m sure that quite a lot of Chinese even appreciate Lady Gaga. Sadly, not many seem interested in the creativity, innovation, democracy and openness that really make America what it is. ^35

So long as the Chinese can’t sell the idea of wearing changshan to a westerner while they themselves nearly all wear jeans, they have lost the game, and will always be buying products created by someone else. Materialistic people may not care about this now, but it will hit them and their children in the future. So this narrow materialist approach to causes and conditions is not only shallow and inaccurate, but produces considerable suffering and a demeaning sense of inferiority and deprivation. ^36

7. Rinpoche, although Buddhism is flourishing in China in one way, yet we seem to have lost the value system of our society. In fact, not having any real spiritual path or belief system may now be the most serious social problem in Mainland China. ^37

Actually if the Chinese people are careful, China, unlike many other countries, is very fortunate to have a really good value base. I used to be a bit cautious about Confucianism, but I now realize that Confucianism can be a great thing. ^38

For example, I think the warmth, human values, kindness and sense of responsibility I see in the Taiwanese society must be attributed to Confucianism. When you go to American cities like Seattle, within an hour you hear police sirens chasing criminals, whereas in Taiwan you hardly ever hear police siren, only the melody of the garbage collector. How little crime there is there must have something to do with Confucianism, and also with Buddhism of course. ^39

So the Chinese should really treasure Confucian values, but the wisdom of Buddhism and Taoism should also not be forsaken or forgotten. Probably the government, intellectuals, politicians, business heads and parents still use Confucianism when it suits them, but they do seem to forget about Taoism and Buddhism. ^40

Right now, there are a lot of rumours and also evidence about the Chinese becoming very materialistic. But there are also so many touching events and stories of individuals undertaking amazing activities to help others, especially when events like earthquakes, floods and other disasters strike. ^41

This indicates there is an ever-present humanness in the Chinese society that we need to cultivate more by putting it in school textbooks, for example. Similarly, I have also heard that there are still many people who go into retreat at Mount Zhongnan and other mountains. It may be hard to believe now, but it is actually these people who will keep the unique Chinese character and values alive 200 years from now. ^42

There are also individuals who bought a whole town and made it into a heritage or cultural preservation site. This shows me that these people have seen something of much greater value than just piling up money. ^43

I am not opposed to people wearing Rolex watches or buying expensive shoes and bags. But they should also plant the seeds of spiritual value and cultivate them, because without those values, they’ll become shallow cheap beings who can be bought, and they’ll unknowingly develop a society that is corruptible and inferior. ^44

Spiritual seeds can be planted through small things, from appreciating Chinese tea incense all the way to reviving China’s ancient hermitage culture. ^45

8. Some friends of ours are very nice people who try to help others whenever they can. But they don’t feel the need to study or practice the Buddhadharma simply because they think their behaviour already conforms to Buddha’s teachings. Is it possible for them to be liberated just by being kind-hearted without any study and practice? ^46

To be nice, kind, and compassionate, and to help others is truly awesome. But it’s not the only thing, and in fact is not the ultimate aim of the Buddhadharma. We practice Buddhism not just to become a good person, but to liberate ourselves, to see the truth and to enable others also to see the truth. After all, there are so many kind and compassionate people who are not Buddhists. ^47

In fact, even among animals, you see kind, compassionate and concerned behaviour. And some who call themselves Buddhists, even over a long period, are not kind or compassionate, but camouflage their selfish behaviour with a Buddhist identity. ^48

And even those who sometimes act kindly and compassionately find it really difficult to maintain and to be patient. For example, I consider myself quite patient, but Parisian taxi drivers are known to be the worst and treat their passengers horribly as if they’re giving them a free ride. So one night in Paris, I was in a taxi with a really rude driver who would drive fast and then slam on the brakes so hard that I got hurt. I got so mad and lost my patience, but I didn’t know what to do because I was in the back seat, where I just peed right where I was sitting. ^49

So even someone like me who was born a Buddhist is not always patient. ^50

9. We have friends who are young and healthy with stable happy families and to whom samsara[5] seems enjoyable. So it is hard for them even to think about renunciation. Could Rinpoche please address this? ^51

Forget those people who cannot think of renunciation.Even I myself, who supposedly teaches renunciation, forget that every moment I am aging and getting closer to death. My good health is like a dewdrop on the grass, dependent on so many conditions, many of which are not within my own control. ^52

It is difficult for many of us to know that samsara is suffering, because our projections of life are always through the lens of hope. For instance, ^53

even when we actually do suffer, we believe that our problems can be fixed. Yet how many times, when we have fixed our problems, have new problems arisen? But still, this thought that one day we will settle down somewhere where there is no problem remains so strong with us. ^54

So renunciation mind is difficult for everyone. But if we have it, renunciation mind can actually make our lives richer, and help us appreciate what we have much more, from family, friends and wealth even to a simple cup of tea.  That’s because someone who thinks of impermanence will drink a cup of tea as if it is his last, and thus enabling him to appreciate the tea so much more. ^55

10. Some of our friends respect the Buddhadharma, but haven’t taken refuge[6], because they worry that the Buddhist precepts will deprive them of the freedom to do whatever they want. Could Rinpoche please address this? ^56

First of all, fear of losing freedom is very normal. Everybody is afraid of that. Ironically, however, no one really knows how to be free or how to free themselves ⎯ whether from a cult guru, from a very stubbornly embedded cultural tradition like ancestor worship, from a religion like Buddhism or Confucianism, or from a political belief like communism, socialism or democracy. ^57

We always end up entangling ourselves in wanting something to hold onto, and many times we don’t realize that we’ve been caught and bound by that. Scientists, for example, point out that religious people are bound by dogma, and they think they are more liberated from such beliefs. But actually they are also caught by a certain system. ^58

So I think that kind of apprehension of losing freedom is very understandable for those who don’t understand that Buddhism is actually a path of liberation. As well, Buddhism for generations, and even centuries, has been completely and almost unavoidably immersed in certain cultures. Those cultural traditions have often created some kind of system or code of conduct that might also feel constraining to those who mistake the culture for the essence of Buddhism. ^59

So it's very understandable that these people do not wish to get into some kind of vow-taking commitment. But if they can understand even a little bit what Buddhism is all about ⎯ for instance that taking refuge in the dharma means taking refuge in a truth like the impermanence of all compounded phenomena ⎯ they might feel liberated by accepting a truth rather than feeling bound or constrained by a code of conduct or the rituals of a club. ^60

In fact, they might see that if they don't accept such truths and take refuge in them, they will end up blindly believing that many things are permanent, which in turn leads us to suffering. For example, if you take refuge in the truth that if we are born, therefore we are going to die, then I think that even the way you eat, shop and talk to people will be different, because suddenly the knowledge of that truth hovering over you actually makes you a bit humble and very genuine. Probably you will even look at your boyfriend or girlfriend in a different way, because when you see him or her as impermanent and your relationship also as impermanent, this might actually lead to a much more harmonious and precious relationship. ^61

But to come back to the question of why some people hesitate to take refuge in Buddhism because they fear they might lose their freedom, I’d like to point out one more thing: due to a lack of proper understanding of the dharma, people immediately think that to be Buddhist means to become a monk, or go to a cave, or become vegetarian, and never again to taste a gin and tonic or enjoy silky lingerie. This is not true. You can do all of that. People should remember that there used to be Buddhists like King Ashoka[7] who had much more than gin and tonic, and Kublai Khan who had much more than a New York rib steak. ^62

11. Some of our friends have a strong belief in modern science and technology, so they always ask whether the Buddhist view and theory can be proved through a scientific approach. Could Rinpoche please talk about the relationship between Buddhism and science? ^63

One thing we have to know is that when you say “scientifically proven”, you are always talking about a mind involvement. When you say we are proving something, someone is proving that to someone else’s mind. No matter what we are investigating, proving or not proving something is always happening within the sphere of mind. So, at the end of the day, it is your mind that is making a decision on what is true or not true. For me, religion and a science are equally superstitions. ^64

The definition of science is “intellectual and practical activity encompassing the systematic study of the structure and behaviour of the physical and natural world through observation and experiment.” If that is the case, Buddhists will say that Buddhism is most certainly science, science that was taught 2,500 years ago through systematic study, experiment and observation of phenomena. ^65

It is important for people to know that 2,500 years before Einstein found time and space to be relative, the Buddha had already taught that time and space are relative. And 2,500 years before the scientific world became critical towards the notion of an almighty creator, the Buddha had already taught that a creator God does not exist. ^66

Buddhists believe things never appear randomly but always have causes and conditions, which is surely the core approach of the so-called scientific world today. But I want to say that the observation of causes and conditions in Buddhism is actually much deeper and more internal than in modern science, because Buddhists are not primarily interested in an emphasis on external phenomena modern scientists tend to be. ^67

Of course, if you have time, energy and the resources to study and practice the dharma, that's excellent. But if not, then even managing to take refuge once is good. And if you have not forsaken or given up the meaning of or abandoned the Three Jewels, you are still much better off than ordinary people who maybe rich and successful. ^69

Chapter 2: About Cause, Effect and Samsara ^70

13. Does samsara really exist? ^71

Samsara means going round and round. But what is it exactly that is going round and round? It is ignorance[9], action and its result (based on the 12 interdependent originations) ⎯ each one creating the other. And as a result, samsara seems to exist so vividly, painfully, tangibly and colourfully, in a way that is both orderly and chaotic. And in that way, just like a nightmare, samsara exists. In fact, it exists so systematically that we even forget that samsara is an illusion. That's how powerfully samsara exists and functions. So it’s not something we should kid around with. ^72

14. How do you define “self” in Buddhism? ^73

Buddhists define self as an imputed idea that comes from looking at a transitory collection of five aggregates[10] ⎯ form, feeling, conception, impulse and consciousness ⎯ and then calling it “self”.  Even though it is imputed, one should not take this lightly because, as I said earlier, this so-called “self” is so well and systematically imputed, and we are so used to it, that we have even forgotten that it is imputed. It has become a habit. ^74

It’s as if you have a habit of chewing something sour, so that when somebody else chews something sour, you immediately get saliva in your mouth, even though that sour taste isn’t even in your own mouth. That’s how powerfully habit works. So in Buddhism, self exists relatively ⎯ as a habit and as an imputed notion. ^75

15. If there is a next life, who will be living my next life? Will that be the“current me”? ^76

If I come to your door tomorrow, we assume that the very same “me” who is here today is also coming tomorrow.  Just like that, on the relative level, we have a so-called next life into which the imputed self comes, just like on the next day. ^77

The current “me”is just an abstract idea. It is nothing concrete or substantial. Yet you can’t really say that yesterday’s “me”and today’s “me”are the same thing. If they were exactly the same, then we would always be stuck with the same “self”, and there would be no change or possibility of change. We could not improve, experience changes within us, or even forget or remember things, and there would be no need to shave or cut our hair. ^78

However, we also can’t say that yesterday’s “me” and today’s “me” are completely different, because if they were two separate beings like Obama and Osama, then it would be like Osama farting because Obama ate too many beans. In fact, it’s Obama who farts after eating beans, which shows there is also some connection. ^79

In other words, yesterday’s me and today’s me are neither the same nor different. Continuity is a total illusion, but a very powerful illusion. Without it, what we did yesterday would have no impact on today’s me. But we human beings do have habits, and so we come up with the idea that there is a continuum from yesterday’s me that will also continue to the next life. And this illusion is strong enough for us to hold on to, and to become the vessel for pain, suffering, joy and all our other emotions. ^80

16. But today’s me remembers yesterday’s me, while next life’s me doesn’t remember its previous life. ^81

If something drastic happens, like the ceiling falling on your head and giving you a concussion, you suddenly won’t remember who you are, let alone what happened yesterday or ten years ago. Most of us don’t remember our past lives because something even more shocking than the ceiling falling has happened ⎯ something called death that caused a concussion big enough to separate the mind completely from the body. ^82

But even if we do remember our previous lives, that still does not prove that past lives actually exist, because what we remember comes from the mind, so it is just imagination. But at the same time, even though we may not remember who we were or our address and parents, we still have a lot of habitual hang-ups and familiarities that may be stronger than remembering. For example, for no obvious reason we may feel emotionally or psychologically attracted or disgusted towards someone ⎯ feelings that may appear to carry forward even when we don’t remember who we are. ^83

I have a friend from Europe, a fashion designer, whose wife is so beautiful that others admire her and envy him. She has beautiful red hair, always clean, keeps her nails nicely polished, and is very elegant. ^84

But this fellow recently met an Australian woman ⎯ as old as his mother and with none of his wife’s qualities ⎯ who, when she speaks, I always secretly wish she would use Listerine(breath fresher). Yet, he is so attracted to her and says she is the only woman who has really turned him on in all his life. When I ask him what he likes about her, he has hundreds of reasons, but you can see they have no logic. His attraction is really just a feeling that’s now become a logic in his head. ^85

Feelings like that in this present life are actually much stronger and more habitual than memories, and may therefore shape our so-called next lives when we have no memory of who we were. ^86

17. About the process of samsara, the teaching on the twelve links of interdependent origination[11] is difficult to understand and remember. Could Rinpoche talk about the process of death and rebirth in your own language? ^87

The twelve links of interdependent origination ⎯ called the twelve nidanas in Sanskrit ⎯ is a very important but very complicated teaching. It is a very sophisticated layout of how cause and effect work ⎯ how a cause gives birth to an effect that in turn becomes the cause of the next effect. It’s the continuation of this circle of cause and effect that we call samsara, and its discontinuation that we call nirvana[12]. ^88

So the twelve links of interdependent origination provides a really important diagram to tackle the root cause of the problems and pain that we suffer. To put it very simply, when we look at something like an orange, we first experience the orange as a whole, not as parts like skin, juice, pulp, pits, etc. We also see the orange as if it were an independent entity, not as dependent on a whole range of conditions like the orange tree, sun, soil, fruit-picker, etc. And we experience the orange as if it were a permanent entity, not as a momentarily existing phenomenon that will either rot and get mouldy or be mixed with saliva, digested, and excreted as urine and shit. So basically you have a wrong idea of that phenomenon you call “orange”, and that is the core delusion that produces the suffering of samsara. ^89

And that misapprehension then causes the next set of nidanas, which are set in motion once you have the delusion of the orange as whole, independent and permanent entity. That delusion gives rise to emotions like craving for the orange, hoping for the orange and fearing that you won’t get the orange, and to actions like trying to get the orange. All these lead you to the birth of the orange, which in turn leads you to the death of the orange. ^90

This is of course a very brief, generalized and simple way of talking about the very profound teachings of the twelve links of interdependent origination. ^91

So the strategy for cutting this chain of cause and effect is first to analyse where the pain of losing or not getting the orange we want comes from — namely from craving for and acting to get the orange. Then as we analyse further, we realize that this craving itself begins with the delusion of looking at something as an entity called “orange”, so we try to tackle that. ^92

Once that delusion is eliminated, hope and fear for the orange cannot arise, the chain of craving and grasping stops, and birth and death of the orange will also stop. Then, once you are liberated from the delusion of thinking that the orange is real, there is no more reincarnation. ^93

18. What is the Buddhist view of fate? Can fate be changed? ^94

Fate is a very tricky word. If fate and karma are interpreted as meaning the same, then of course Buddhism believes in fate. But the word “fate”, as generally used in English, seems to have the connotation of being beyond a person’s control, and of being predetermined by a power beyond human influence. In that case, fate is definitely not the same as karma, and is not in line with the Buddhist understanding. ^95

The Buddhist view is like this. Suppose you are playing cards, you always have the initial choice not to deal the cards and not to play. But once the cards are dealt and once you are playing the game, you have to follow the rules of the game. ^96

Another example is you can always not cook the egg. Once you start cooking it, you still have the opportunity not to finish cooking it. But once the egg is 99% cooked, then it is difficult to have the egg only 50% cooked because you’ve already created and set in motion certain causes, conditions and actions that will produce a result. ^97

So Buddhists will not agree that somebody else or some supernatural cause designed this fate and pre-fixed the outcome long ago. You have the power to have the kind of fate you want to have. It’s up to you. So one can say, karma is both free will and fate, so long as fate is understood to be the result of certain causes and conditions we’ve previously created, rather than being predetermined by forces outside our control. ^98

There is also the continuous habit of ignorance, which seems so pervasive that it almost feels like fate; but actually it is not. We only feel it to be fate because we have had certain habits for a long time. Everything we think of as being our so-called “fate” is actually something we ourselves produced through our ignorance. But then we forget that we ourselves made it, and much later we think it’s just our “fate” that somehow randomly happened to us and for which we are not to be blamed or held responsible. ^99

19. So if we can then change so-called “fate” through dharma practice, what kind of life should we aim to live as Buddhists? ^100

As Buddhists, our fundamental and sole aim is to understand the truth. So whatever we do is really to get closer to that truth. But there is also the continuous habit of ignorance, which is very deep and strong. We long ago chose not to want or be attracted to the truth, but to find comfort in what is illusory and untrue. So we’ve now become so accustomed to these illusions that pursuing the truth seems painful, boring and unprofitable. ^101

This is why the compassion of sublime beings for sentient beings is so profound and amazing. Sentient beings function this way — they hardly ever do anything to get closer to the truth, but they do a lot of things that take them further away from the truth until they feel doomed by “fate”. ^102

Yet sublime beings, through their great compassion and wisdom, know such outcomes are not outside our control, and they do everything to point to the truth through teachings and demonstration. ^103

We think our fate is like moths being attracted to light and jumping into it, and we don’t know how to tell ourselves or others we’ll die there. So we give up, and think there’s no point doing things differently. But sublime beings don’t see us like moths whose so-called “fate” is inevitable, because they know we can always choose to dispel ignorance through training. ^104

It’s tragic that most of us choose not to have that tough, boring training because we are so pulled and pushed by all sorts of entertainments that attract us just like moths flying into the light and getting killed. ^105

20. Earlier you talked about Buddhism being very scientific in relying on systematic study, experiment and observation. But are modern science and technology now changing the laws of cause and condition? According to Buddhism, for example, practicing patience in this life will make us beautiful in the next life. But nowadays people change their looks through cosmetic operations, and scientists are modifying genes to change aspects of our basic biological nature. How does the law of cause and condition function here? ^106

The fact that scientists are now able to make the nose bigger, lips smaller and chin sharper is itself a function of the law of karma. So, hypothetically speaking, if Marilyn Monroe is considered the ultimate beauty and if such beauty were to bring ultimate happiness, then you could say we have better karma today than 50 years ago because plastic surgeons are much better able to make you look like Marilyn Monroe. ^107

On the other hand, even looking exactly like Marilyn Monroe does not guarantee happiness. After all, she herself suffered severe depression and died mysteriously at age 36 in a “probable suicide”. As well, some might not find Marilyn herself beautiful. And even the best plastic surgeon cannot make people like or dislike a certain look. Often in fact people despise those who had plastic surgery and say they can’t smile any more. ^108

So beauty and ugliness are still dependent on the beholder, the subject, and that in turn is very dependent on karma, as is the uncertainty of equating a particular look with happiness. ^109

Moreover, even if such surgery could change the effect, such as making someone more beautiful, one still needs the conditions to do the surgery, to ensure its success, and preferably not to cause unwanted side effects. So the law of cause and condition still fully applies, and is certainly not superseded by advances in science. ^110

I once saw a beautiful girl in a train station in India, with the most slender, curvy, tall, dark body. But she was a beggar, the right side of her face was completely disfigured, and she had no money for food, let alone plastic surgery. In her case, she did not have the karma to access the surgical treatment that might have made her a supermodel. ^111

21. Rinpoche, could you explain why there are people who did wrong to others but still live a very good life? ^112

First, you never really know if they are having such a good life, because that’s only an appearance ⎯ our own perception. And what is a good and successful life anyway? Is it being powerful? Was Hitler really successful in having so much power and in causing so much pain to others and eventually to himself? Is that kind of power a “good life”? ^113

If you ask Jigme Lingpa[13] about a very rich Californian who drives a Porsche, lives in a multi-million dollar Malibu cliff house overlooking the sea, and just surfs all day long and parties all night, he would think it’s such a bad karma for someone to squander his precious life like a complete fool. Conventionally of course, we think what a good time this rich Californian is having and how fortunate he is ⎯ an image fostered by Vanity Affair, soap operas and more. ^114

So the definitions of success, wealth, power and a “good life” are so relative. For instance, people generally think wealth is a good thing⎯ and it can be if it’s used to create real benefit. But it can also be really troublesome: how many people have deadly disputes because of wealth? How many children fight each other for an inheritance after their parents die? How many countries have waged war for oil? There are so many problems because of wealth. ^115

Generally in Asia, we think having lots of gold comes from having a lot of merit. But that’s a very cultural understanding, and we really have to understand what we mean by luck or merit. ^116

We never know whether having a lot of money is lucky, or whether it’s a good thing that the economy is booming everywhere, because in the long run that booming economy could destroy the earth. We don’t know if it’s good that someone from Shenzhen can drink fresh kiwi juice from New Zealand, when the pollution from that long-distance transportation may cause lung cancer in your beloved granddaughter. ^117

So in Buddhism, wealth, good fortune and a good life are measured by having the chance to see the truth. So a mighty and rich king like Ashoka and an ascetic like Milarepa[14] who barely has enough to eat are equally venerated because they both saw the truth. We don’t prefer Ashoka just because he has more gold coins than Milarepa. ^118

22. In history, there are people who aspired to justice and even went to war and killed people in the name of justice. What kind of karma will they get because of that? ^119

One person’s justice is often another person’s injustice. Mostly, fighting for justice, freedom, equality or minority rights — though it might look admirable and inspiring at first glance — has a blind side and is based on a very narrow view. We might be fighting for our village or province or race or nation, but it’s often at others’ expense. ^120

If we take a big view of justice for the whole world, we won’t please anyone. Our kinfolk will be upset that we are not giving them preference, and even our enemy will think we’re playing a trick on them. But from that big view, justice is one of the biggest jokes and hoaxes on earth, because improving some people’s lives is not only at the expense of other people (like the horrible conditions of the cheap labourers who make our fashion jeans) but of other species and the whole planet. ^121

From a Buddhist point of view, anything that derives from ignorance and is lack of wisdom, whether in the name of justice, equality or anything else, will create karma, whether good or bad. For a Buddhist, the ultimate aim is not to make more karma of any kind, bad or good, but to be free from all karma. ^122

23. But aren’t justice, freedom, peace, equality, minority rights, filial piety and other such values good to pursue? ^123

Samsara is imperfect by nature ⎯ not just some parts of samsara but every single part of it. Strictly speaking, from a dharmic perspective, even the traditionally virtuous values like justice, freedom, peace, equality, love, family bonds, filial piety and so on, which are usually celebrated, are said to be imperfect, because fundamentally all these values are derived from ignorance. ^124

We noted above that one person’s justice might be completely unfair for someone else. Similarly, one person’s freedom is often another’s slavery; equality for some means deprivation for others; and peace for one nation is oppression for another. So these values are really relative, conditional and changeable. ^125

Some things in samsara that appear good may only be good for the time being. But if you plant a poisonous seed, it will grow to be a poisonous plant, even if it looks beautiful and even if it does not harm now. One of these days, this plant will damage us. This is how samsara is. ^126

For example, filial piety and family bonds are very venerated in Asia. You almost don’t need old folks’ homes in places like Taiwan, because family members will take care of the old folks. In the United States, there are old mothers who have successful millionaire children, but the poor mothers are alone or in old people’s homes, because all their successful children are so busy making more money that they have no time for their mothers. In a culture that worships individual rights rather than family bonds, filial piety does not have room. ^127

This problem that seems more prominent in the west is no longer just in the west. It is actually becoming a modern global problem, and is even coming to remote places like Bhutan. In the modern world, values such as family bonds are no longer cherished, which ironically fuels loneliness, boredom and alienation, which then lead to many other problems like drug abuse and crime. ^128

But even though samsaric virtues like filial piety and family bonds are good in one way, they are also the root of many bad things, and are the source of a lot of corruption and nepotism. In so many countries, like Indonesia, India and others, things get ruined because a president gives rights to all the lucrative businesses to his own son or cousin, or a Prime Minister gives a profitable road-building contract to his wife’s distant cousin. All that happens because of filial piety. That is why nothing in samsara is perfect, and it’s why justice and the other values in samsara are also not perfect. ^129

I want to give you one more example of how tricky such good samsaric values can be. When I was growing up, I used to be so puzzled that my grandfather would express such serious concerns about one of his students who seemed to be such a good person. This student was not only well off and from a good family, but was easy to get along with,  had a good and upright character, and was always kind and helping others. Even from a spiritual perspective, people might consider him a bodhisattva. ^130

At that time, I thought it strange that my grandfather expressed so much more worry and concern about this good, successful person and his dharma practice than about other students who seemed much less good and successful, who didn’t practice, who were not so kind and helpful, and who had far fewer advantages. Even when this student seemed to be doing everything right, my grandfather would snarl at him and be so critical, insinuating and disapproving. ^131

But now it makes sense to me, because sometimes such success and good human qualities can become a dangerous sidetrack that ends up becoming the aim of life and distracts us from the higher goal of seeing the truth. In fact, this good-natured student had a retreat hut that I don’t think he ever spent more than a week inside, because he was so popular that people were always coming to see him. So now I understand why my grandfather was so much more worried about him than about others, and why he really wanted him to practice to achieve something much higher. ^132

24. Can we obtain happiness, fortune and good health through the practice of the Buddhadharma? If so, can we make samsara a happy existence without having to pursue liberation? ^133

There is no doubt that the ultimate aim of the Buddhadharma is liberation. For every technique and method of the Buddhadharma, if it does not bring you worldly happiness, it is at least guaranteed not to bring you misery. We don’t even know the definition of “happiness” most of the time. But if we analyse, we find that broadly speaking, happiness has so much to do with control ⎯ we think we are happy when our own life is under our control. ^134

When we need to depend on someone or something else, let us say financially or emotionally, we always feel like we are dependent on that person or condition. And feeling we need to rely on someone fundamentally makes us feel that we have no control of ourselves or our own lives. That is usually not a happy situation. ^135

Health problems have a lot to do with stress, especially in our modern life. How do we tackle stress? Lavender oil might help a little bit, but at the end of the day it really comes down to how much control we have. To be more explicit, we mostly hope blindly and unreasonably for the outcomes we want, so we are bound to be disappointed and therefore stressed when we don’t get what we want. But when a person has control, he or she may have a hope, but there is also another thought inside saying “this may not happen”, and this kind of awareness will prepare one for the opposite of what is hoped. ^136

The most fundamental techniques of the Buddhadharma, which enable us to contemplate loving kindness and understand the truth, should give us the means to gain some control over ourselves. I don’t think we will find something else that can better make us happy, wealthy and healthy than the Buddhadharma. ^137

25. If the karma accumulated in previous lives is stored in the alaya[15] and passed down to this life through the mind stream[16], can karma also be passed to us through our family lineage? In other words, if we inherit many attributes like bodily features, personality, and even mindset from past lives, does that mean karma can also be inherited through family? ^138

Buddha said he cannot wipe off someone else’s suffering or share his liberation. It is totally up to individual hard work. So you cannot inherit, delete or download karma from someone else. But the fact that you are born in a family means you have a karmic link with family members. There is a group karma that is inter-linked. Using that link, one can influence someone else’s karma. For example, with a person from Kazakhstan, who is also a follower of the Buddha and whom I have never met before, I have the karmic link of both being followers of the Buddha. ^139

Generally, as human beings we have similar karma, and then beings born in the same continent, nation, province, and then village share ever more intimate karmic links with others. Of course, then it gets even narrower, because we have very intimate karmic links with some of these people who become our father, mother, wife, husband and so on. ^140

So you can say that all Americans have the group karma of having George Washington as their first president. Similarly, North Koreans have the group karma to have Kim Jong-un and previously his father Kim Jong-il as leaders. Accordingly, these two nations function and exist as they do. ^141

26. Could Rinpoche please talk about collective karma[17]? ^142

Collective karma is like the karma shared by the victims of the tsunami in Japan, for instance. Some of the victims may not actually be from Fukushima, but may just have been visiting there for that hour and been trapped, because those particular visitors had similar karma to go through the same kinds of consequences as the actual residents of the area. Likewise, during the tsunami, there were people, who not only didn’t die, but surprisingly didn’t even get a scratch. ^143

Another example is that many of the Arabs have the collective karma to have oil, and yet we don't know whether that’s a good thing or a bad thing. On the one hand, it makes them so wealthy, but on the other hand, it causes them a lot of problems like war. ^144

It’s hard for many to accept that seemingly “innocent” people suffer painful collective results. But from the Buddhist point of view, we have to understand that no one is innocent. If you are a samsaric being, you are not innocent, and the very fact that you are a samsaric being means that you have done something that caused you to be here. ^145

27. We often heard the Mahayana vow that we will remain “till the enlightenment of all sentient beings.” Is such an aspiration really possible? There are so many sentient beings to liberate. Do the bodhisattvas say such things just to comfort us? ^146

Generally, we use such phrases and make such ambitious and far-reaching vows in part as technical training of the mind, so that the bodhisattvas will have a boundless intention and view, and thereby generate incredible courage. Right from the beginning, a bodhisattva has to train himself or herself to think on a grand scale, so therefore in the Mahayana prayers we never hear a wish for only this village to be enlightened, but instead we hear:“May all sentient beings be enlightened.” ^147

On the more subtle level, what do we mean by “all”? That is something that we need to contemplate, because “all” is nothing frightening or fearful. “All” is just a concept. If it is easy to help one sentient being, it can be just as easy to help all sentient beings. The concept of “all”, or infinite, is very abstract. ^148

For those who are not good bodhisattvas, like myself, even “one” is frightening. “All” to me is not as frightening as “one”, because it is too abstract. ^149

28. Living with karmic debt makes me feel so nervous, so how do we know whether or not our karmic debt has been purified? ^150

When we don’t have to rely on any causes and conditions, then we are no longer a victim of karmic debt. For instance, if you feel that you need to have the love of your friends to make yourself happy, that means you are collecting karmic debt. ^151

If we are fishing for praise, if we get bothered by criticism, if we feel lonely when people ignore us, and if we get excited when we become famous, then we are consciously or unconsciously always looking for comfort or security. ^152

Then we have this fear of not being happy, and we work so hard and put so much effort into gaining praise, avoiding criticism, gaining attention and avoiding being ignored. As long as you are doing these things, you are still bound by karma, and therefore you are bound by karmic debt and its effect. ^153

29. Which level of practice must we reach in order not to create karma anymore? ^154

That is a very profound question. In the sutras, you will hear expressions like “I have done my deeds”, or “I have shrugged off my load”, which mean that there is no more karma and karmic consequences. Broadly speaking, when you reach the first bhumi, then you are most likely beginning to be free from creating karmic debt that will drag you to the lower realms. However, you are still relying on virtuous karmic deeds to bring you closer to full enlightenment. ^155

When will the fire extinguish? When the wood is totally exhausted. As the wood gets less and less, the fire also gets smaller. Because of insecurity, we tend to add more wood. As you become more advanced on your path, the effort of not adding wood will increase. So naturally, when fire doesn’t have fuel, it becomes less and less. ^156

Chapter 3: About Mind Training and Practice ^157

30. What do you think is the meaning or definition of being mentally healthy? Are we all a little mentally ill in one way or another? ^158

In psychology, I think there is quite a difference between what is considered mentally healthy or not. In Buddhism, the understanding of the truth and the realization of the truth is what makes your mind healthy. The more you know the truth and the more you realize the truth, the healthier you are. In fact, in one tradition of Buddhism, which is called Mahasandhi[18], to shrug off and get rid of the mind altogether is what is healthy, because mind by nature is not healthy and is something to get rid of. From that point of view, as long as you have a dualistic mind[19], you are not healthy. ^159

31. What makes you think that mind can be tamed? What is the essence of mind training? ^160

Mind can be tamed because mind can be influenced, and this is probably the best news that we have. You can experiment with it. For example, even if you like to drink alcohol a lot, if you put enough effort into not drinking, you can always stop drinking for a night or two. That actually proves that you can go against mind or for mind. So that means mind is trainable and tameable. Mind can be tamed because mind is a by-product ⎯ it is a compounded phenomenon. ^161

Whatever we want, we can sort of force ourselves not to want, even for just a few minutes. Madhyamikans[20] always use examples like this. So what we are saying here is that we can prove mind is trainable in two ways. Suppose you have never liked paan (betel), but if I keep on giving it to you, you will end up liking it. On the other hand, let’s say you like paan and then I talk about how bad paan is for you, then you can easily get disgusted with paan. ^162

All this proves that mind is trainable, and this applies to our emotions, and to the notions of friend and enemy, and other concepts. So our anger, our rigid mind and our concept of enemy are all by-products of training. We were trained to get angry and jealous ⎯ that’s why we are so good at it. And so of course, such trainings and tendencies can also be reversed, and we can instead train our minds to be loving, kind and compassionate. ^163

But the real essence of mind training, or rather to put it more accurately, the purpose of mind training, is really to understand the truth of everything. At the moment, it is the mind that keeps on believing something fake and illusory to be real. And that’s called delusion, which can be dismantled through mind training. ^164

32. We know there are so many ways to train the mind. In fact, every method in Buddhism is to train the mind. But in a nutshell, what is the most practical mind training that one can apply? ^165

There are two basic methods. The first one is designed to make the mind malleable, because at the moment the mind is rigid and not at all malleable. You could say that mind has its own mind at the moment. So we have to make that mind flexible and pliable. ^166

33. So how should one do this? ^167

Contrary to what you may be thinking ⎯ which is that to make something malleable we need to soften it by adding liquid so it becomes more bendable ⎯ here we do the opposite. We have to learn to focus and to focus long. That’s key to making the mind malleable. ^168

For example, you can look at your toe right now ⎯ look at it for more than two minutes, and really focus on it. You will realize that you cannot. You become restless. You will want to do something else, like WeChatting. And when your mind wants to WeChat, that’s the time your spoiled brat-like mind is wailing like a child. Keep on looking at the toe. It will wail more. Don’t give a damn about it. It’s now one minute and thirty seconds. You really want to WeChat. Keep on looking at your toe. And now your mind’s wailing will change. It will make you think about nail polish or colour. Be careful. It’s just changing the tone of its wailing. Just look at the toe until two minutes are up. ^169

If you can keep on doing this every day, your mind will become like the dough that’s been kneaded to make into Chinese noodles. ^170

34. What is the other method? ^171

The other method is much more complicated. Its purpose is not just to make the mind malleable, but now you want the mind to see the truth. And mind really doesn’t want to see that truth. So for that, you need all the training of hearing, contemplation and instructions. ^172

35. Could you tell us why aspiration is very much emphasized and could you tell us how to practice aspiration? ^173

The mental and psychological character of aspiration is longing, which of course is one of the strongest human habits. And we beings have habits, which are actually the dominant ingredients of sentient beings. Many of these habits, we don’t even know or recognize as habits because they have become our reality ⎯ very concrete, as if they are the real thing. ^174

Habits like drinking coffee in the morning, we consider as habits. But from the point of view of Buddhist psychology or metaphysics, like the abhidharma[21], those are very gross habits. In fact, this carpet, for example, your nearest mountain and river and so on ⎯ all of these are also habits. ^175

I am not talking here about us having a habit of using carpet in our house, but I am actually saying that the concreteness, the material, the hardness, softness, pattern, colour and shape are all habits. I am not only saying that you have the habit of seeing this as a carpet, but that the carpet itself is the habit. ^176

That is how Buddhist metaphysics sees it. So we consider drinking coffee in the morning as a habit, but we think that the carpet, the spoon or Hong Kong are real, and that they are actually there. But from a Buddhist point of view, these are also habits. ^177

These apparently concrete things like mountains, rivers, house, carpet and a glass of water are nothing other than habits gone fat. They are so touchable, wearable, cut-able and drinkable that they now become concrete and “real”. ^178

Identity, religion, language and culture ⎯ all these are habits gone fat. When habits are shared by a lot of people, then of course they become even bigger and fatter. Then they become almost indestructible, and we become convinced that even though we will die, the Great Wall of China will continue. I am telling you this, because it has something to do with what we will discuss later. ^179

Among the habits gone fat, one of the most vicious, most organized and most powerful, is the body. One of the reasons it is so vicious is that it is so fragile. The great Longchenpa describes the body shell as being like an eggshell. An eggshell is very organized, intact, so smooth, and no water can get into it, but it is so fragile. Similarly, the seeming solidity of this fat habit of body is really a tough habit, and gets even more evident and tough when someone steps on your toe. ^180

When I was in Bhutan recently, I went to a really remote place where there were so many practitioners gathered. It was a very long walk ⎯ many hours for many days. If I myself had even a small headache, I had to take medicine, try to massage myself, rest, and all that. There was this one yogi I saw, with his feet cut and just a makeshift bandage, walking uphill carrying lots of luggage, without even noticing his wound. ^181

Then of course, fashion, vanity, and things like Calvin Klein don’t help make this fat habit of body any thinner. On the contrary, they make this fat reality even fatter. ^182

The point here is that habit is all we have ⎯ both as a cause and as a result. According to the Tantra[22], after you die ⎯ when all our elements[23] and senses and sense objects[24] start to dissolve ⎯ as time goes by, the habit of this body disintegrates. ^183

So for example, right now I see my shoes as shoes and wear them accordingly. But when I die, after a few days, as the habit of my body disintegrates, I will not know what shoes are ⎯ especially if I am going to be reborn as a parrot in the Amazon. Then what are these shoes? The shoe habit is gone; the element of being human has ended; and a new habit is about to arrive. ^184

So now, going back to your question about aspiration, especially in Mahayana, we begin by understanding that everything is just a habit, as we discussed. Even the Buddhist path is a habit ⎯ a new guided, monitored habit to counter the old habit, because we know that the old habit is giving us problems. ^185

One of these habits that we have is called longing. We will long for something no matter what, and we long for so many things. There is always a longing there ⎯ longing for companionship, longing for a good view, longing for fresh air ⎯ there is always some kind of longing. ^186

Then one day when you wake up, you might have a longing to know what to long for. That is good, and that kind of longing is probably a turning point for you. Most of the things we presently long for are deceptive and delusional. We are mostly longing to troubleshoot something temporary. So this is why knowing the right thing to long for and to aspire for becomes crucial. ^187

We are presently aspiring for seemingly very mundane things, like to be majestic, to look good, and to be healthy and prosperous. But actually we mainly long to know the right thing to long for ⎯ such as longing to go under the bodhi tree to get enlightened. ^188

And from the tantric point of view, we really long to see everything as deity[25], mantra[26] and mudra[27].We long to know where we are right this very moment, and we aspire to know constantly this very moment mind and to see phenomena as they really are. ^189

The bardo prayer aspiration is a very profound aspiration. You aspire to grab the chance when you are dying, not because you have been such a great practitioner, but because you are now dying, and so your mind is being left alone and naked mind is happening without you needing to try hard. This is why the yogis pay extra attention at this time, because this is a very important chance. ^190

I don’t know how to put this properly, but it is like panic. Panic is almost like a turning point. It is during the panic that you either develop a new bad habit or a really good habit, like a good discovery. Panic can be a blessing. Panic makes you so naked and so vulnerable. When you are so vulnerable, it is such a good opportunity. ^191

It is the partial vulnerability that is a curse, because it always gives you some sort of stupid blind hope. There is always some stupid railing that you can hold on to. But when there is nothing to hold on to, then it is a good opportunity to face the referencelessness. ^192

And here again, aspiration is probably the only thing that you know how to do. But if you don’t know how to aspire for the right thing, then even the aspiration to have a right aspiration is taught in the Mahayana sutras. You can say: “Even as lord Manjushri and the bodhisattva Samatabhadra aspired, so may I also aspire. May I aspire to have aspirations for all the profound methods of Mahayana, such as love, compassion[28], bodhicitta[29] and so on.” ^193

There are practice methods on the path that are unfathomable, like meditation on shunyata[30]. For most of us, emptiness kind of makes sense intellectually, but the moment you try to do it, the method always sort of defeats the purpose, and you end up doing the opposite. How do you meditate that everything is emptiness? If we think that nothing exists, that is nihilism[31]. In the sutras, Buddha said, mountain-like ego is bad, but it is still much better than a sesame seed-like attachment to emptiness. So the best thing that we can do is aspiration ⎯ aspiration to understand the emptiness in its full complete meaning. ^194

When you study, intellectually you can scrutinize phenomena through the logic of shunyata; but when you practice, the aspiration to understand that maybe the best beginning. So you aspire to realize the unfathomable, unimaginable methods such as shunyata and the guru devotion of Tantrayana[32] in their full and complete meaning, potential and function. ^195

Another unfathomable thing that we are supposedly to practice is benefitting sentient beings. We don’t know what that means for small narrow-minded people like ourselves. It is very nice to say “may I be able to benefit sentient beings,” and that is a politically correct and good thing to say in public. But how are you going to practice that, and how are you even going to begin, when most of the time we cannot even benefit one person all the way? Mostly we don’t even know what the problem is, let alone how to help. ^196

So, unfathomable actions such as benefitting sentient beings can also be achievable through aspiration. Then other things like how do we defeat laziness are also difficult, and more likely we are not going to be successful. So again, we have the aspiration again and again to become diligent and to defeat laziness. ^197

Another challenge, especially for those who are dwelling on the spiritual path, is that inspiration is the key, yet we often don’t get inspired to practice. We don’t see enough people who inspire us and we don’t meet enough situations to inspire us. So again, we can aspire to be inspired. ^198

Intellectually, we know that self-cherishing is not good; and even in the mundane world, a selfish person is not considered to be good. But how not to be selfish is difficult. Even a practice like wanting to benefit all sentient beings may have this selfishness at the bottom. ^199

Self is lurking there somewhere. If you try to be gentle with it, it can get spoiled. If you try to be rough with this self, you may manage to fish this self out of its hideout successfully, but it always ends up finding another hole to hide in. And it has learned all your tricks, while you only know a few of its tricks. So aspiring to defeat self-clinging is a good foundation. ^200

Your aspiration must be accompanied by the right view, but for most of us, we don’t know what that means. For beginners, in order to establish that right view, our aspiration must have a big vision. For example, the Mahayana aspiration is never to be enlightened till all beings are liberated, and that you and you alone are going to enlighten all sentient beings. We are not only aspiring to build many hospitals and feed lots of people, but eventually to make others see the truth. ^201

And the motivation of the aspiration, because of the view, should also be limitless. We should not be limited to personal gains, or small gains, or temporarily solving problems, but having the grand aspiration really to enlighten all sentient beings. ^202

Such grand motivation and vision protect us from disillusionment and disappointment. If you are making a million dollars running a business, a few hundred dollars will not shatter you. ^203

Then discipline of aspiration, which is really I would say the quintessence of aspiration, is to do it with consistency. I am not talking about long and elaborate prayers, but really learning to get into the habit of aspiring here, there and everywhere, in whatever situation you are in, whether high or low, good or bad, success or failure. Never limit yourself to doing the aspiration only in front of a shrine, or holy place or on top of a mountain, but really do it in the most unlikely places. ^204

If you are a follower of the Buddhadharma, I think it helps if you can think of Buddhas and bodhisattvas in front of you, next to you, behind you and all around you, as a support for your aspirations. There are so many reasons why we do this. I mean, just to think of the Buddhas and bodhisattvas has so much merit, and merit is like a fertilizer for aspiration. So you invoke the blessings of the Buddhas and bodhisattvas to actualize what you are aspiring for, to keep your aspirations on track, and not to get swayed by all kinds of other distractions or goals. ^205

And then invoking the presence of the Buddhas and bodhisattvas almost as witnesses when you do your aspirations also seems to generate a sense of duty and to create a certain discipline. Now that you have aspired in front of all the sublime beings, it is your aspiration to go all the way. This is briefly a sort of standard aspiration theory. ^206

36. What does an enlightened person look like? How would his life change if he or she were without dualistic mind? ^207

Indescribable. They might look even more human than us. Or they might appear to be the most short-tempered or the most fussy, or they might appear to be hypochondriacs who are always saying they’re sick. We never know. ^208

Well, being dualist people ourselves, we will never really know how or what kind of life an enlightened being has. But if a person has reached the stage where things like praise, criticism, gain, loss, attention or being ignored don’t affect you any more, then we can only guess what kind of life this person has. I think it is good to try to guess. ^209

37. Have you seen an enlightened being? Is there an enlightened being that we can use as a role model? ^210

Individually I’d like to believe that I have seen enlightened beings — they are my gurus.  That is my practice to see them as enlightened beings. Whether they are or not, is not even an issue. I have to see them as enlightened beings, that is my practice. ^211

I am stressing this out because the so-called right role model is very subjective. Buddha has been seen as an enlightened being by many, but his own cousin and his cousin's followers have not seen him as the perfect role model. And to think that someone is not the right role model is our own perception.  Buddha  said, "only a person like me can judge if someone is enlightened or not. " ^212

This is why we always come back to using our own emotional intelligence and intellectual intelligence. We need to have hearing and contemplation to enhance our intellectual intelligence, and to have meditation to enhance our emotional intelligence, so to speak. Even the very concept of enlightenment, for most of us, is an arbitrary, vague and intellectual idea. Enlightenment can't be bad, it better be good. For most of us, our attraction to enlightenment is maybe because of our dissatisfaction with what we have in this life. So we are looking for something higher, which if you are a follower of a spiritual path, is a good thing — not to be settled with what you have and this mediocre, materialistic world, but really aiming higher and beyond is a good thing. That kind of longing and quest is what makes you a noble and spiritual person. ^213

38. From the Buddhist view, what is real luck? Could Rinpoche talk about merit and luck? ^214

I never understand what the definition of luck is. It seems like the general understanding of luck is that it is something that just comes randomly, out of nowhere. Buddhists do not believe in that kind of luck. In Buddhism, so-called merit is something you can create, and something you can destroy. It does not come randomly. When it comes, it may appear as if it’s random, but that’s only because you just haven’t understood the fine points of karma. ^215

39. What is the relationship between hearing, contemplation and meditation? During different stages of our practice, how do we allocate our time and effort between these three aspects? ^216

Hearing is always so important, especially in the beginning. Hearing and contemplation are still important, even when you are beginning to practice. Once you have confidence in whatever path you have chosen, then maybe hearing and contemplation would be a waste of time, and you should just meditate and practice. ^217

40. What do you think about the practice of life release? ^218

Life release is considered very virtuous and meritorious. There are two kinds of merit that Buddhists talk about. One is called stained, and the other stainless. Stainless merit is the merit you accumulate through devotion to the Buddha, especially meditating, abiding in the present, not rushing after past experiences or anticipating the future, appreciating and probably hearing, contemplating and meditating on emptiness, such as in the Vajracchedika Sutra. In fact, the highest stainless merit is basically contemplating and meditating on emptiness. ^219

When we talk about stained merit, then many sutras and shastras say that releasing life, saving someone else’s life, and saving somebody who is certain to die or suffer untimely death are the highest meritorious acts. In that way, one can also say it is more meritorious than building a temple. ^220

In this day and age, we have new challenges in light of questions raised by environmentalists and the knowledge we now have about the environment and the life style of earth’s inhabitants. ^221

Generally in all Buddhist practices, especially in Mahayana Buddhism, it is the motivation that makes an act virtuous or non-virtuous. You can even be telling a lie with a really good motivation, and you may be accumulating merit instead of bad karma. Based on this, in the past, the motivation of life release was purely to save the life of a being that was about to be killed. ^222

But now we have this new information that certain fish caught in one part of the river may not survive in another part of the river or ocean. With this new knowledge, we have new doubts and ask what the point of releasing fish is if we know they are going to die by putting them in another part of the ocean ⎯ that would almost be like putting the fish in a frying pan. ^223

We also have questions about whether the act of releasing life encourages people to catch more fish, birds or other animals, so that it becomes a means of livelihood for some people. This could be true in places like Burma, Sri Lanka and Buddhist pilgrimage places, where you often see people selling small birds or fishes outside the temples. So there is that argument. ^224

Fundamentally we have to think this way: we have to use ourselves as an example. If you are caught by somebody, by another force, and you are about to be fried, chopped or minced, and you are caged. If someone comes and relieves you from that, even for a few hours, how would you feel? After all, this person is giving you a chance to meet your friends, to go home so you can have your favourite tea, read your favourite book or listen to your favourite music ⎯ enjoyments that you almost lost until this person came. ^225

We human beings have this arrogance to think that animals, like prawns or lobsters ⎯ just because they don’t have an address or phone number, and they cannot complain ⎯ don’t have feelings. So somehow, unconsciously, we think that it is all right to chop them up no differently than we would chop a mushroom. But we all know that if you catch prawns or fish, they struggle and try to release themselves. That must mean that they love their lives and that what they are experiencing is not something they want. ^226

When we look at bigger animals like cows, we can clearly see not only that they love their children, but in fact develop love and adoration for their owner ⎯ a human being ⎯ even though there is not a single means of communication. So they clearly have feelings. ^227

We cannot even bear a small bite from a mosquito, even though the mosquito has no idea that it is you it’s biting. For the mosquito, it is a big opportunity to live its life, and it may only have a day’s life. But we get annoyed, protect our babies frantically from mosquitoes, and destroy them en masse. They are defenceless, while we have defences like mosquito repellent and mosquito nets. The animals are defenceless. Even for a human being with integrity, the act of killing or an action that causes indirect killing is always non-virtuous. ^228

But with all the new information we have, we now have a challenge. While we should try our best to save lives in the traditional way, I think it is time for us to think differently to save lives in many other ways. ^229

If an advertising gimmick from Nike can make the whole world buy Nike shoes, we could easily create an advertising campaign to protect animals and also to promote a vegetarian lifestyle. Then you are not only tackling the problem from the root, but if you intended such a campaign as a form of life release, it could also be a very skilful way of saving life. ^230

In any case, our scientists and physicians have warned us that human beings as a species are generally not meant to eat meat, and that it is actually not natural for humans to do so. And doctors have also warned us that eating meat is not advisable even for your own health, especially nowadays, with all the processed meat with its additives, and how they treat and what they feed the cattle, pigs, chickens and other animals that are raised to be killed on the animal farms. ^231

There is always a question about Tibetan lamas eating meat, which is an old Tibetan habit that will not die easily. But Tibetans now have no more excuse to continue with that habit, and they actually have all the reasons to discontinue it. Maybe in the past, Tibetans either had no vegetables or no knowledge of edible vegetables or of how to farm them. But now with all the great new technologies, you can even farm vegetables on the highest mountains. So unless the lama is a great mahasiddha and yogi who is benefitting beings in whatever he does, the Tibetan habit of eating meat is purely an old habit, and not a good one. ^232

There is also the question often raised about whether Vajrayana allows people to eat meat. Absolutely not. Rather, Vajrayana is about no preference. In the past, in India where Buddhism came from, the brahmanic culture was the highest culture, which looked down severely at meat eaters as the lowest caste. Tantra is always against dualism and is therefore against preferences, which is why in the tantric rituals, we find substances such as meat and alcohol. But people should realize that the context of having meat there is a deliberate pulling the rug out from under practitioners’ feet that worked 2,000 years ago in India’s brahmanic culture. ^233

Chapter 4: Study and Practice of Buddhism and our Normal Lives ^234

41. Will Buddhist study and practice affect our normal lives and work? ^235

Monica works as a lap dancer in one of the sleekest gentlemen’s clubs. As a hostess she has to keep her body beautiful and sexy, which is challenging, because the job requires her to work at night when everybody is networking and when men get off work and go to these kinds of places. She works quite a lot, starting at 10pm at night and finishing around 3 or 4am on weekdays and often later on weekends. ^236

Monica has tried many ways to get other jobs. She is actually an artist and also a trained gardener, but in this day and age it is so difficult for her to get a proper job with decent pay. Besides, there is a conflict, because she is a follower of the Buddhadharma, and her only wish is really to spend more time practicing the dharma. She is really hoping to save enough money for a long retreat, like the three-year retreat. But that requires considerable savings, and to simply live in the west costs a lot of money just for day to day living expenditures. ^237

Monica has considered living and doing a long retreat in a third world country where living expenditures are very low, but there she has encountered other challenges, like not being able to get a visa for a long extended stay. ^238

In her present work, on the other hand, she can make money much more easily than in all the other ways she has tried. In fact, her family is quite wealthy, but they do not want to support her because she is now a Buddhist and they are not. So if she wants to become a practitioner, she has to support herself. ^239

I have noticed that Monica has a constant guilt doing what she is doing, because her work involves a lot of seduction and saying things she doesn’t want to say just to please these men. She also knows that some of these men may be very lonely and depressed, and that she is probably taking advantage of them. So she has a constant struggle, wondering whether she is doing the right thing from a karmic point of view. ^240

For me, as someone who is supposedly spiritually mentoring her, it is actually very touching that she is really thinking like this, because she is not driven by the blind greed that drives our consumer society. Indeed, in our globalized world, it is actually so difficult now to find a means of living that is completely not harming others. The jeans we are wearing that we consumers demand to be so cheaply made depend on cheap labour in third world countries, where our garments are stitched by kids who have no chance of an education, let alone the experience of an ice cream. They are just living to make ends meet. ^241

So in this context, let’s come back to the question of whether dharma practice affects our lives and work. As an entertainer, Monica constantly has to make herself look nice. Her hair needs to be washed and flowing, and she has to have a proper diet. The other girls in her line of work all have to do this and, without a dharma connection, discovering even one new wrinkle is like the end of the world. But for Monica, although her job also requires her to look out for every wrinkle, she is much readier to accept it because of the dharma she practices and studies. ^242

So Monica still continues hopping around some of the most cosmopolitan cities like London and New York. ^243

But I believe that her study and practice of the dharma has a very positive effect in her life and work. Often we have this fear that dharma practice means we have to give up our life and work. But that is not necessarily true. It actually brings a change of attitude, and a change in how you deal with life, work, and most importantly, death. ^244

I like the fact that as soon as Monica has enough resources, she just practices. She never thinks about saving money or buying the best bag, jewellery or earrings. Every penny she saves is just for practice. It actually makes me feel ashamed, because I have so many conditions to practice the dharma that I am quite spoiled. As well, Monica genuinely tries to have the aspiration that for whomever she is contacting, through that karmic link, she will eventually benefit them. And I cannot argue with that. ^245

Some of my most profound conversations have been with Monica. For example, her job requires her to paint fantasies with these old rich men, but as a Buddhist she also tells them that it’s her job to do so, even though admitting that is not good for her job because she is quite beautiful and the men also fall for her. In my assessment, Monica’s struggle is one of the most profound spiritual struggles, because looking at myself, I wonder if I am always as honest as she in what I say wearing a Rinpoche’s outfit. Many times I make promises to all these lost and needy people in the name of skilful means, saying things that are not true, like “it should be all right, don’t worry.” ^246

My other friend, Pat Armstrong, was a computer programmer and software designer who had done lots of practice for a long time as a Buddhist student. When she found she had a fatal disease, I told her:“You are dying now. This is the reality. You have to face it”. She died from cancer, and I believe she would not have taken it so well without the dharma. So yes, the practice of the dharma does not mean that you have to give up your life and work. Rather the practice of the dharma means you can deal better with unforeseen changes in your life and work. ^247

Even if you are a CEO of the biggest company in the world, that does not mean you have bypassed or transcended death, old age and sickness. You are just as vulnerable to old age, sickness and death as anyone, if not more so. Yet I don’t think many of the CEOs of the big companies are ready to accept these realities. But what is the point of building a life so diligently and making it into something so fragile and petty, if we are not ready to accept such basic realities. ^248

Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche’s disciple Gerard Godet was one of the most accomplished engineers of his time, making quite a fortune from his talents. Not only did Gerard dedicate his fortune to setting up meditation centres and building monasteries, but he himself did almost nine years of retreat towards the end of his life, and died in retreat. He was probably one of the happiest men I have met in my life. ^249

But if we are not practicing, then just being in a monastery, wearing robes, doing mere rituals and customs, or even receiving some vows may not change anything. ^250

42. Is taking refuge in Buddhism one way to escape from reality? ^251

What is reality? What we have in the material world⎯ideas, possessions, friends, families, relationships ⎯ are these reality? Are these everlasting? Are these certain? From the dharma’s point of view, they are not. They are ever changing. And change is inevitable. ^252

Although we all long for or work towards some sort of permanence, certainty and happiness, it is just not going to happen. It has not happened yet to anyone. There is no record in human history that someone has lived happily forever. That’s only in the movies. Yet we do keep on thinking that this exists. What greater illusion can one get than that? It is an absolute illusion. ^253

Actually what Buddhists do is to escape from that illusion, so Buddhism is facing reality, not escaping. People may think reality is saving money, getting a Prada bag, getting a better job, having a lot of possessions, but some of us know, even if only intellectually, that this is not reality. The habit of longing for such kinds of things and seeing illusion as reality is strong. ^254

I am sure for many people, Buddhism is looked upon as escaping from reality. But actually very few can escape from the kinds of illusions we mistakenly think of as reality. If you can escape from these materialist illusions, you have achieved a lot. Our forefathers, the Buddha, Shantideva[33]and others, were not homeless bums. In fact, both the Buddha and Shantideva were princes, sons of kings, and had the highest levels of luxury and amenities in their royal living situations. But they saw those as illusory and painful, and they escaped from those. ^255

So when we take refuge in the Buddha, we are escaping from that delusion. We have to start by acknowledging that we have certain delusions and benchmarks that have been forced on us by our governments, our families and our culture. In this materialist age particularly, we are measured and we measure ourselves by how much we have, completely forgetting who we really are. As a result, we become so boring and clichéd, with everyone wearing the same thing and aiming for the same illusory goals. ^256

Imagine that in a village, all they sell is hamburgers. You go to one shop, and that is it. Human beings are becoming very boring. If it were only boring, it might be fine, but it is also painful. We don’t realize we have made ourselves so boring, but on the contrary, we like to think that we are all so special. The way we measure how special we are is by who has the fastest car or who has the most money or power. ^257

By contrast, taking refuge in the Buddha fundamentally means accepting ourselves for who we really are and seeing our fundamental nature as the Buddha. Among many other advantages, imagine how much this will relieve you from all sorts of unnecessary assumptions, and from all sorts of hopes and fears, like the hope of being invited and the fear of being excluded from a certain party, dinner or clubs, for example. ^258

I don’t know whether any of you ever roamed the streets of Shanghai or Beijing, for once just being yourself, not being measured by how much you have or by whom you mingle with and what kinds of friends you have,  but simply as a human. Maybe you should try that, even just for a few hours once in a while. Maybe you will see so many beautiful things. Because so far what we see and what we hear are through the filter of hope and fear. Even combing or not combing your hair is done because of hope and fear. ^259

All these inhibitions caused by measuring whom we are connected to create so much stress. No amount of spa treat mentor massage can help, because there is so much stress. And people are not even sexy or elegant, because they are so stuck up and so stiff, and  not relaxed. Girls who are not conscious of their beauty are the most beautiful. If we look at these big models or film stars who are almost constantly stressed or worried about how they appear in the public, we don’t see any charm and elegance. In fact, even when we look at them, we ourselves get stressed. ^260

Take myself as an example, as a Rinpoche and reincarnation of a very respected and known master, I end up living and functioning in the world of hypocrisy almost all the time. What I like or dislike, what I prefer or not prefer ⎯ all these preferences I end up not expressing, because there is a certain amount of pressure from the society to appear a certain way. ^261

Taking refuge in the dharma means accepting the truth. Everybody wants to be healthy, wealthy and happy. But if we can accept the truth that no matter how hard we try, we will definitely get sick, die and leave all our wealth behind, we won’t get so stressed trying to save and accumulate more and more. And if we accept the truth that we are absolutely alone, we will not go out socializing that much, because there is no point. When you are socializing with a table full of people, they are all individuals. Nobody knows what the next person is thinking or up to, or what he or she is hiding. ^262

Somebody working as CEO of a big company like Boeing will probably never think that death can come at any moment, or that there is brain tumour growing in his or her most beloved child’s head. Or they never think they’ll have an accident that will put them in a wheel chair the rest of their lives, missing all the parties their friends throw. ^263

But also when a good thing happens ⎯ like winning the lottery, meeting a nice friend or being successful in business⎯ we never really know how to appreciate it. Immediately we get greedy about how to make it better, how to invest it to get more and how to invade more, or how to seduce the friend. ^264

To be a spiritual person means to be able to face the truth and surrender to that truth, be it good or bad. When good things happen, appreciate them. When bad things happen, don’t get too carried away or too dismayed and hysterical. That is the essential meaning of taking refuge in the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha. ^265

Of course, in order to enhance that practice of taking refuge, it always helps to have a shrine, to have a statue of the Buddha, to go to a temple, to actually recite the refuge prayers, and to think about the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha in whichever form or way is possible at whatever time. There is no reason why we cannot think about the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha even while we are dancing on the dance floor. ^266

43. Do we have to abandon all of the enjoyments in our lives in order to practice Buddhist meditation? ^267

First, it's important really to know what we mean by enjoyment. When we say enjoyment, what is it we are enjoying and is it really enjoyment? For example, we used to enjoy sand castles, small toy cars and Barbie dolls, but perhaps not any more. So we used to enjoy things that we don't enjoy anymore, and we’re now enjoying things we never used to enjoy. As well, what we are enjoying now we might not enjoy later. ^268

We also don't know whether what we are enjoying now is going to give us all kinds of problems later. For example, we might be so enjoying eating ice cream but is it good for us? We may be enjoying eating fried chips, but that may give us high blood pressure that shortens our lives and kills us. ^269

We may be enjoying political power, but that can put us in prison and get us executed. So before answering the question of whether we have to give up our enjoyments in order to practice the Buddhadharma, it is important first really to come down to asking “what is enjoyment”? Things that we consider enjoyment,  are they really enjoyment? And if so, for how long? And at what cost? These are important questions to ask to oneself. ^270

Often, we work hard for 11 months and then we take a break for one month to go to Hawaii. But that's just how the world is designed these days because of our greed. We work so hard so that we can have a good time during the weekend. When you look at this from a different angle, a lot of the things that we do are really ridiculous. ^271

Do we like to have a very shiny car, and then constantly worry that it will get bumped or scratched or stolen? And do we like to have a beautiful appearance and then go through all this constant suffering of not eating and exercising and all that. ^272

On the other hand, if enjoyment is having a simple life, good health, enough food to eat, shelter, and probably a companion, then Buddhism does not necessarily require you to get rid of those. In order to practice Buddhism, you don't have to abandon any real enjoyment. In fact it is the spiritual path that makes you enjoy your life more. It even teaches you how to enjoy more soberly, nicely and economically. ^273

In fact, Buddhism teaches you not only how to enjoy yourself, but at the same time not to create harm to others, and even to make others also enjoy their lives. For instance, generosity is one thing that is taught and greatly emphasized by the Buddha, and it is also very much practiced in China where there is such a good habit of generosity. ^274

If we give someone a dollar, not for show or competitiveness but genuinely and out of real kindness, we might think it will make the receiver happy. But what we forget is that it mostly makes us happy as a giver, because it is such a wonderful feeling to be able to help someone and to make a difference in someone’s life. Your dollar might even save someone’s life, which makes the giver so happy. ^275

You can try this yourself. When you give, does that give you instant joy, just from the very act of giving? Of course you will have even more joy when you see the face of the receiver, because when they are happy, you get happier. ^276

So, if you think about it from all these angles, you can see that Buddhism is not at all a path to abandon joy and enjoyments. On the contrary, it is really a smart way to enjoy oneself. ^277

So as a follower of the Buddha, it is not as if one should not go to parties, and it is not necessarily true that we should only eat very cheap or bad food. Asceticism comes in many different ways. ^278

44. So is it okay to watch TV and movies and to read novels, or is that a waste of time? ^279

If you have enrolled yourself to do a retreat or intensive study session, then yes, you may not want to watch TV and read novels during that time. But generally speaking, if you deprive yourself of these things, you may end up having a lot of constipation, both metaphorically and literally. So I would not emphasize giving these things up, unless you want to become ascetic, which most of us have no ability to do. ^280

The Buddha told his disciples that dharma practice should be like playing the lute ⎯ it should not be too tight or too loose. I have seen people who take pride in supposedly not indulging in entertainment, but they are full of themselves with pride and are condescending to others. I have also met people who ebb their lives away watching TV and reading novels, but they are kind and generous and constantly feel guilty for not practicing. I almost prefer the second ones. ^281

45. What is the Buddhist attitude towards worldly achievement and earning money? ^282

It is not money or worldly success that is the problem. That's never the problem. It is the greed and attachment to money or success that is the problem. If you have so much attachment to money and success, then no matter how much you have, you will always think you don't have enough. That's how it works. ^283

So, Buddhism does not see money, power, position, a good car, a Rolex watch or gold plated teeth as a problem. In fact, if you want to plate all 28 teeth in your mouth gold, Buddhism is not against that. Buddhism is against the attachment to that. ^284

Also, there may be people who want to pursue worldly success so that they can actually help more. In that case, the more worldly success and gain achieved the better. But, here you have to be careful, because worldly gain and success are very intoxicating. You may begin with a good intention to help people, but then get caught by the game of worldly success. And when that happens, instead of you controlling worldly affairs, they will control you. ^285

46. Should we aspire to become a businessman or businesswoman? ^286

A business career can be very helpful to the world. In fact, I would say that in this day and age, if we could create a good business community, that would probably be one of the most crucial contributors to the world. ^287

Some people think that Buddhism is against the business world, but that is entirely not true. Even in the Buddhist sutras, there are a lot of wealthy merchants like Anathapindika who became very important patrons and benefactors of the Buddhadharma. ^288

Like any other career, including the job that I do that is supposedly teaching the dharma to people, if a businessperson’s motivation is wrong and if there is pride and selfishness involved, it will destroy the world. At the same time, I think that what Bill Gates, Warren Buffett and others have achieved through their charitable contributions is admirable, and we should all rejoice in that. ^289

Unfortunately, business usually reaps its profits by diminishing or undermining something else, like the natural environment. So I think the future generation of business people should really learn to make their money in a win-win situation, rather than at the expense of others. The business community could have a much bigger vision and plan than at present. At the very least, even if one is selfish, one could think of one’s own great-grandchildren and how they will survive on this earth. ^290

47. Should one aspire to be a politician, and if so, how? ^291

Like the business community, I think that politicians can play a very important role. I think it’s unfortunate that people generally have a negative view of politicians; and of course it’s most unfortunate that in many parts of the world, politicians have done a lot of bad things that merit such distrust. ^292

But politics with the right motivation and action can be very helpful indeed. There are leaders like Mahatma Gandhi, Vaclav Havel, Nelson Mandela, F.W. de Klerk (who freed Mandela and abandoned apartheid) and José Mujica, the Uruguayan president, who have done an incredible job and set a real example. ^293

Historically too, there are great leaders and politicians who have brought happiness, peace and contentment to thousands and even millions of people. Because politicians have so much power, they really can influence events positively and make the world a better place. ^294

Unfortunately, because of the general belief that politicians are all bad, it seems that many young and smart people, often the cream of the crop, now go into business. And so we end up with not such smart people in politics. ^295

48. In the business and political worlds, it’s sometimes so difficult to work with people. As a Buddhist businessman, is it okay to fire someone? As a politician, how should I lead? Should I be tough? How should one associate with difficult colleagues? ^296

To be Buddhist does not mean to be weak and unorganized. You can always pretend that you lose your temper; and anyway not to spoil others is also helping others. So if you need to scold, or even give punishment, you should. But it’s really important to contemplate and observe how much this is coming from your own ignorance, greed and blindness. You may not necessarily be clear that it’s actually coming from your greed, pride and actual temper ⎯ you may not realize that. But just genuinely asking that question alone will do wonders. ^297

49. Should one become a musician, artist or actor? ^298

As with business people and politicians, the right action triggered by the right motivation can contribute a lot to society through the arts. Such motivation can be seemingly simple and mundane, like an actor’s wish that whoever sees him or her on screen or stage will somehow experience genuine love, compassion, peace and harmony, and even see the truth. And a musician similarly can have this wish for whoever hears the sound of his or her music. ^299

As abstract as this sounds, having such motivation will genuinely have an impact if you can really have thoughts and intentions like this in your heart. Even if that impact does not materialize right away, it will definitely plant a seed that will eventually grow, ripen, and create real benefit. ^300

50. Besides having the right motivation and action, are there concrete practices (like chanting particular mantras) that businessmen, politicians, actors, musicians and others should do? ^301

There are many good practices. For those who are not Buddhist, I would suggest that they should keep aside a time, even as short as three minutes a day, really to be alone and contemplate.  During this period, you should switch off your phone, not look at email, not watch TC, pick up a magazine or respond to others’ demands. ^302

Instead of all those normal activities, just sit somewhere quietly and observe your body. Have you consciously noticed that you have two legs? I know you know this theoretically, but just notice and observe them directly and consciously. Do you remember that you have a mole on the right side of your upper lip? ^303

Or one day, instead of observing the body, you can contemplate your feelings. You may be feeling agitated or excited or drowsy or bored. Don’t make any judgement but just observe. This quiet contemplation and observation for a few minutes a day is something anyone can do, and I really believe it can help a lot. ^304

If you are a Buddhist, then of course you can remind yourself a lot of Buddha’s teachings. If you are a leader, then remind yourself that other people have feelings, greed and problems just like you do, and generate a good motivation, such as an aspiration to be helpful to the world. ^305

And if you want to chant something, then it’s always good to chant the mantra of dependent arising: Om ye dharma hetu-prabhava hetum tesham tathagato hyavadat tesham cha yo nirodha evam vadi mahashramanah svaha. ^306

51. Sometimes our daily work keeps us very busy and in constant contact with many sophisticated people. Can we practice in this case? ^307

Like a lotus growing in muddy water, you do your daily work without getting stained by it. In fact, the lotus actually makes the muddy water look nice too. ^308

For certain reasons, some of us have jobs or responsibilities that require us to get acquainted with people, and so we need to dress well, go to posh restaurants and do all sorts of things that are futile. But a practitioner who knows the uselessness of all these things may still do them in order to serve a higher purpose like benefiting others, or even just because it is your job. Doing it while knowing that it is absurd is what Buddhists call skilful means. ^309

So a practitioner will mingle with these people the way a wounded person might mingle in a crowd ⎯ always watching out, not falling into people’s games, and yet not making others upset. You can play along with their games without getting caught by their games, and in the process you might even manage to inspire others. ^310

52. In the ritual of taking refuge, there are phrases about avoiding the companionship of Tirthikas[34]. How should a Buddhist treat friends who are atheists, Taoists, Confucians, and from other religions? ^311

Certainly this phrase does not mean a Buddhist should not hang around with a Christian, Jew or Muslim, or go out for a drink or to a coffee shop with them in a friendly human to human way. All that is fine. What this injunction in the refuge vow means is that you should not have such a spiritual companion who would influence you spiritually. This is because some of these religions believe and talk quite differently from Buddhism. ^312

For instance Buddhists believe in karma, whereas Christians, Jews and Muslims believe in an almighty creator. These are two very different views. Buddhists believe in reincarnation, they don't. Buddhists believe in non-duality, while their view is very dualistic. So if you want to go to the left and have definitively decided to do so by taking refuge, someone who follows the path to the right should not influence you away from your chosen direction; otherwise you will be confused. ^313

Here we are not judging other religions to be inferior to Buddhism. We are simply saying that they are fundamentally different, and you cannot follow both at the same time. ^314

I have encountered people who believe in a particular path that they claim to be a mixture of Christianity, Buddhism, Taoism and more. I think it is a very nice experiment done with good intention, but not only is it not going to work, it is also a disservice to humankind. It is better to have variety, like Chinese food, Indian food and French food, so that people can choose their dish. If you mix them up in the name of harmony, you just end up making a bad dish. ^315

As for Confucianism, I understand Confucianism is more like social ethics and etiquette that is about how to live your life. Confucianism may not be interested in enlightenment. In that case, I don’t see any major contradiction: you can be a Buddhist but still use Confucian values. I can see lots of benefit to being with a good Taoist scholar and practitioner, but to mix Taoism and Buddhism is not necessary, and again is a disservice to each. ^316

53. Does one have to go to monastery and become a monk or nun to study or practice Buddhadharma? Under what conditions should one leave home to practice and under what conditions should one practice at home as a lay person? ^317

Some people think that to study and practice Buddhism perfectly, you basically have to renounce everything and go to the forest or a cave or become a monk or a nun. If you think that way, you are really limiting the Buddhadharma. Because the Buddha was such an omniscient and omnipotent being; he gave a path that is so infinitely rich, that it is good for anyone under any condition, be it soldier, merchant, king, queen or whatever you are. This is important to realize. ^318

To renounce family life is one out of many methods⎯ out of 84,000 methods in fact. Such renunciation is a very admirable, respectable and inspirational path, which is surely to be encouraged. But it doesn’t mean that it is the only way. ^319

Moreover, it’s very important that we know the reason why we renounce things like family and worldly life. It is not because they are evil, but because they are an illusion. Understanding that is the ultimate renunciation, just as renouncing pride, jealousy, anger and other afflictive emotions is the core purpose of renunciation. Becoming a monk or a nun does not necessarily guarantee that kind of ultimate and core renunciation. ^320

In fact, as many people surely know, spiritual materialism has always been prevalent, and in fact, spiritual materialism is much more dangerous than material materialism. This is why you see some of the abbots and monks of big monasteries becoming very rich, powerful, greedy and arrogant, even though they are labelled as supposed renunciants. I would actually prefer Al Caponeto these monks and lamas who pretend they are renunciants, because they have so much hypocrisy that they even believe in it themselves, which is really dangerous. ^321

Now under what condition and situation should one renounce something? The time to do that is whenever you really see the downfall and illusory aspect of whatever you are renouncing, and feel real revulsion towards it. At that time, in fact, you have already begun renouncing. ^322

Chapter 5: About Emotion, Love, Relationship, Marriage and Ego-clinging ^323

54. According to Buddhism, what is the nature of emotions? ^324

In the conventional sense, emotion is a mental factor which is based on a mistaken perception or misunderstanding ⎯ basically looking at things that are impermanent and seeing them as permanent; looking at lots of parts and particulars but seeing them as a whole and one; looking at something that is dependent but seeing it as independent; and looking at something that is totally transitory but thinking it has a very solid “self”. This on the conventional level is the nature of emotion and is how the emotions function. ^325

But on the absolute level, as Shantideva put it very beautifully, the nature of emotion is none other than wisdom. When emotion is functioning just as conventional emotion, as a mental factor based on fundamental misunderstanding, then it is like water functioning as ice ⎯ it is frozen into fixed notions of permanence, solidity, independence and self-ness. But when there is no longer any such misunderstanding, then there is no ice, and such fixed notions have melted to become water functioning in an entirely different way. ^326

It’s a bit like the annoying human character trait of fussiness, which at other times might be considered positively as meticulous attention to detail. So, for example, the very emotion we call anger, when properly used, can become mirror-like wisdom. ^327

55. Is there a way to get liberated from loneliness, fear, and frustration? And until then, how can we better face loneliness, fear and frustration? ^328

“Self” never has a concrete base. It is an imputed idea, and therefore a fundamentally mistaken idea, like looking at a scarecrow and thinking it is human. But as ridiculously illusory as this sounds, the habit of clinging to the self is so strong. This so-called “self” seems so concrete⎯ so big, so small, so sharp, so blunt, so tall, so short, so sensitive, so insensitive. But the fact of the matter is that it has no base. ^329

So it’s almost like being homeless and having no real home, but refusing to believe that you are homeless. Imagine yourself going into the apartments of people who have gone out of town for holidays, and somehow managing to live there as if you are the inhabitant, even though it’s not your home. You use the sofa, fridge, shower, towel and all that, you watch the TV and even put things back properly, ironing and vacuuming. But fundamentally it’s not your home, and any moment you will have to leave, because it’s not yours. ^330

That’s how the self feels all the time. By nature it is lonely. And the most stupid thing about it is the big denial that you really have no home. So you have become so bold in this denial , even printing cards with the address of someone else’s apartment to convince others that this is where you live. And of course it is a scary thing to do that, but it is also exciting and even thrilling, especially when others believe your lie. ^331

But that’s how the game of selfishness plays, and of course this makes us lonely. How do we liberate ourselves from this loneliness? The Buddha tells us we just have to accept we have no home and no base. Not only that, but in fact, the very notion of the self, the inhabitant itself, the dweller in the home, does not exist itself. So we cannot even define or qualify ourselves as an inhabitant. But nobody is going to hear this, because that’s the biggest insult. ^332

56. Why has depression become such a serious and worsening problem in modern society? ^333

There must be so many reasons why there is so much depression in society today. But the main reason for depression is that unconsciously, there is this uncertainty hovering in the back of our minds that there is no confirmed identity. So when we say “I”, even though we are so confident about whom we are talking about, if we analyse a bit, we cannot really pinpoint what we are talking about. Because the identity of the “self” is uncertain, we then try to prove ourselves and the existence of this self in so many different ways ⎯ by becoming fashionable, educated, strong, or sometimes even weak. ^334

We also try other things, such as sex, shopping, gold plating our teeth, having liposuctions and making ourselves look like Korean boys. Then we create benchmarks for educational achievement, looks, wealth, size of car, size of penis, boobs, finger, etc. And when we don’t reach those targets, that causes so much depression. And we will never reach where we think we want or ought to be, because we have so much greed. Even if we were there, we still think that we are not. And anyway those very benchmarks themselves are just like ripples in the water. ^335

So having too many benchmarks and too unrealistic benchmarks to reach, we come depressed just trying to reach them, not being able to reach them, not reaching them fast enough, or not reaching enough of them. ^336

57. Psychologists are not very helpful in dealing with depression. But as we know, Buddhism is all about mind training. So is there any effective method provided in Buddhism for depression? ^337

I don’t know much about psychology, but overall, especially in places where there is no culture and tradition of contemplation to look inside rather than just outside, psychology maybe very helpful. And it may also be increasingly useful in a traditional society like China, because the Chinese, in the pursuit of modern wealth, are forgetting their old values like Confucian ethics, sacred temples and hermitage. ^338

I was recently reading a book about the ancient culture of hermitage, and was amazed to read how popular this practice was in China in the past. Those are actually priceless psychological therapies, but are now dying. ^339

We Asians are such suckers to western culture. That’s understandable to a certain extent because western culture is so practical ⎯ like a pair of jeans is easy to wash and dry and doesn’t even need to be ironed, whereas it’s not so easy to travel with a changshan. So in Asia and other places where psychotherapy is a new phenomenon, our new lifestyle may be making it necessary. ^340

But in the end, psychology, like massage, is a method of recreation. Its aim is to make you healthy and “normal”, whatever that means. Buddhism goes much beyond that. Buddhism ultimately does not care about being healthy or normal, but aims for liberation. ^341

In fact, there are so many methods in Buddhism to counter depression, but we have to keep in mind that countering depression is not really the ultimate aim. Rather, as a bonus result, depression can also be tackled. Among the many available techniques, contemplation and mindfulness —, especially methods like shamatha and vipashyana[35] are among the most effective ways to counter depression. ^342

Our world does not contemplate enough or encourage mindfulness. If we do actually contemplate, we usually contemplate on external and materialistic things, and hardly ever on internal or simple things. Contemplating doesn’t have to be intricate or mystical, and being mindful doesn’t have to be moralistic. Rather, contemplating and being mindful can be absolutely straightforward⎯ just a pure simple observation of what is going on right now at this very moment. ^343

58. Earlier you said that to practice Buddhism, you don’t need to abandon enjoyment. But to achieve enlightenment, should all desires be abandoned? ^344

Enjoyment and desire are not the same thing. You can enjoy something just by appreciating it, without wanting to have it or own it⎯ in other words, without getting obsessed with it.  So yes, you don’t have to give up enjoyment, but all desires, including the desire to be enlightened, ultimately do need to be abandoned. ^345

For the time being, as Shantideva said, there is one desire and one ignorance that should be kept as a tool, and that ignorance is thinking that there is enlightenment. And why should we keep that? Because we don't want to suffer. ^346

The whole path of Buddhism, broadly speaking, is like a very skilful arrangement of different courses of medicine. So there is one set of medicine given to ordinary practitioners and bodhisattvas. Then when those ordinary practitioners become not so ordinary, another set of medicine is given to forget the first set, and so forth. Until you are really, really advanced, medicines continue to be given to forget all previous medicines. But in the end, if you still need to take any medicine, that means you are still sick. So the ideal situation is that you should not have to have any medicine at all. That’s why the state of the Buddha is also referred to as the path of no learning. ^347

59. If all emotions are suffering, what is the real happiness in life? Is the life of a Buddhist practitioner very boring? ^348

Everything in life is happiness to a certain extent, even scratching a pimple. It’s just that when you do it too much, it becomes painful. Having a pimple is not a nice thing, but scratching it gives you a certain pleasure, a certain satisfaction. So in this example, because nobody likes to have a pimple, one can scratch it and experience that bliss. ^349

At the same time, to put it very plainly, the so-called happiness that we think and talk about is always a partial suffering. So first we need to ask, if all emotions are suffering, what do we mean by happiness in life? What we generally call happiness in our lives is actually very confusing. It’s a bit like the satisfaction of scratching a pimple, where the pleasure is dependent on just temporary relief of pain, which comes back again after the scratching is over. ^350

It's also like getting married, which supposedly helps relieve your loneliness, but also brings you suffering. Or if you have good food like a nice Italian pasta in front of you, it’s so yummy and delicious, yet even as you eat, you start feeling concerned about the excess carbohydrates, and then a whole slew of worries about your blood pressure, your stomach, your six packs, losing your good looks and more are suddenly all there. ^351

So what we call “happiness” in life is really very imperfect, and quickly spills over into suffering. And yet, even within this imperfect samsara, one could still try to scrape together a little happiness just through generating contentment. And when you are content, you’ll find it’s almost impossible to be bored. It’s when there is so much greed that it’s almost impossible to get stimulated. ^352

60. How does ego function in love and relationship, and what kind of role does ego-clinging play when you’re in love? ^353

Ego always plays the most vicious, clever, deceptive, powerful and destructive roles, but it is most successful at its game when it comes to love and relationship, because everybody falls for it. It is easy to distinguish between shit and gold, even though they are both yellow⎯ you know what to avoid and what to get. But it’s harder to distinguish between brass and gold, because both are shiny and at first glance look very similar. ^354

Love is a bit like that. In ordinary love, there is seemingly so much giving, understanding, patience, forgiveness and creating space. When you are in love with a particular person, you don’t even notice the chewing sounds he makes while eating that usually annoy you to death. Being in love is so deceptive, because it looks quite unselfish, and you can even be very generous. All this makes ego so clever when ego wears the armour of love. ^355

Habit plays a big role in love and relationship. And of course, the loneliness that we talked earlier is a total sucker to this game of ego, which is love. ^356

But having said all this, I don't want to dismiss genuine bonds between human beings, between boys and girls, girls and girls, boys and boys, husband and wife, and mother and children. ^357

At first glance, the love between parents and children is probably the least selfish form of ordinary love. But that too is something to analyse, because children are often our assets and toys, which is very selfish. This is why, fundamentally, the relationship between parents and children is a complicated one. The family gives a lot, but no one likes to be treated as an asset, and children’s antennae subtly pick up such signals that are unconsciously transmitted by the parent. ^358

So it’s very complicated, and we have such a stake in appearing to be generous, giving and unselfish that we are generally afraid to express our selfishness openly.  Suppose I express to the girl I am in love with, that I love you because there are a lot of things that you have that I want and can use, and I’m also willing to give you what you want in the hope that you’ll give me this and that in return. If you say that to somebody, he or she is going to be so annoyed. But if you could actually manage to do that, then you will have a really good relationship. ^359

61. So, can right attitude improve relationship? And if so, what attitude should we develop towards love and marriage? ^360

The right attitude comes from the right view. And the right view, fundamentally, should be based on accepting the truth and not denying the truth. For example, if the Buddhist view that everything is impermanent is deeply rooted in your head, then every moment of your relationship will be cherished and treasured. Such understandings can help nurture proper relationships. ^361

Times have also changed. It’s obvious that the whole institution and purpose of marriage and the way it functions now are very different from what we thought about marriage even a few decades ago. So hanging on to a fantasy of what was considered a perfect marriage a few decades or centuries earlier, and thinking that can happen in this day and age, could lead to lots of misery. But if there is proper agreement and understanding between couples, marriage can be very productive and could help many others. ^362

If you are a Buddhist, and especially if you are following Mahayana practices, then you can also have a family life that benefits many others. You can at least begin by benefitting your own children, and giving them the right influence and education so that they will grow up benefitting many others. So marriage can be a tool or medium that is very beneficial. ^363

62. In the big cities, there are ever more talented, good looking, independent and kind hearted females with pleasant personalities. Yet it is so hard for them to find husbands. What do you think is the reason for this and do you have any suggestion for these women? ^364

This doesn’t have to do with relationships but reflects a fundamental change in our society and samsaric existence. In the past, women were kept in the dark intellectually, chained, bound, and kept in the house. Now women are becoming ever more educated and intelligent, and they want to be independent. ^365

This view and attitude of independence is in their psyches, and even in the shampoo they use. So when women project this colour, light and radiance of independence, the chances of scaring men away are much higher. ^366

But this question also illustrates how relationship is fundamentally a flaw that comes from a mixture of lot of factors. First, trying to find perfection in an imperfect world is challenging. Secondly, there is a bit of contradiction in pursuing independence and simultaneously looking for a life-long partner. And there are also many other factors that, while seemingly small, actually cause major impacts, because everything in our modern world is designed to separate us from each other. ^367

These include changes in place and time. For example, the institution, meaning and value of marriage in a desert or nomadic culture is very different from what is practiced in Shanghai or Beijing. And, as I mentioned before, there have been huge changes just in the past hundred years. ^368

So we read novels written 500 years ago about a couple in a perfect relationship, and we listen to old romantic songs, or read the proverbs of Confucius, for example, on the value of marriage. But here we are in the year 2015, when televisions have so many channels to choose from that they naturally separate husband and wife because each has their own favourite show. ^369

In fact, everything in our modern world, including all the consumer products, isjust pushing us to have separate things, including even separate cars. There are wedding planners who try to help people get married. And then in the same company, there may be people whose job is to divorce people, because divorce is also a good business. When people get divorced, they buy more things because they live separately. ^370

So maybe it is time really to redefine what we mean by marriage. Living happily forever, based on a model of what happened 500 years ago in China, India or elsewhere, can never happen again. Maybe these days we need something like singles ads, dating services, and match-making websites that advertise a beautiful man looking for a beautiful girl to be co-shareholders in a company called marriage. You are lonely and bored, so you want to have a partner, but you are also selfish, so you want to be independent. ^371

Ironically, misfortune sometimes helps the stability of marriage. For example I know a girl with a boyfriend who has epilepsy, so there is a cause to take care of that boy, which somehow binds them together. Many times a couple live nicely together because their child is born handicapped or retarded. ^372

I wish I had a good, simple answer to your question about all these bright young women who can’t find husbands, but unfortunately I don’t. As I’ve mentioned, there are so many different ways to look at this, including the apparent contradiction between wanting independence and also a life-long partner. ^373

Maybe the only general advice I can give modern young women trying to find the right man is not to be too fussy, and also to have a bird’s-eye view of life. Remember that the man whom you are taking as a partner has a mind of his own and therefore has his own habits, some of which he doesn’t even know. So some of those habits might manifest after three years of marriage. ^374

You should also think about what it is that you want out of this marriage. Do you want a child? Is marriage a manufacturing industry for creating children? In that case, if that’s your aim, then it doesn’t matter if your husband listens to Symphony Number 9 or not. ^375

And you should ask yourself whether this man will give you a lot of freedom and space, and whether you will give him freedom and space.  We are quick to say we will give that space, but mostly we don’t. ^376

We should also not be too attached to being with a partner happily forever. Often clinging to this idealistic wish creates so much misery. ^377

At a more basic level, what all this discussion really shows is that samsara is fundamentally flawed: for one thing to seem to work, certain other things would not work. ^378

63. In your recent interview in Southern People Weekly (Nanfang Renwu Zhoukan), you mentioned that you have a girlfriend. This has made a lot of people confused, puzzled and even sad. Are you a monk? ^379

First of all, it’s important to understand that practicing vinaya[36] is not the only way to practice the Buddhadharma.In many traditional societies, people believe that to be a good Buddhist, you have to be a monk. So a lot of people seem to have been influenced to believe this. This is not true. The vinaya path is just one of very many teachings⎯in fact, just one of the 84,000 different methods the Buddha taught ⎯ and there are many other valid ways one can follow. ^380

Even in the Shravakayana[37] school, with its strong vinaya teachings, lay people are never rejected. The Buddha taught a complete path to enlightenment for lay people. That’s absolutely important to know, because otherwise the Buddha’s teaching would be very partial and limited, working only for a certain type of people. Then there would be a big problem, because it would become just like a caste system excluding whole categories of people. ^381

At the other extreme, some people believe that Tantra means there is no vinaya. Such people seem to think, “Oh, if you're a Vajrayana[38] or tantric practitioner, you don’t have to have vinaya , you can drink, have women and wine, and so on." And that is also not true. There are a great many tantric practitioners who also practice the vinaya. ^382

In all cases, the Buddha’s methods, as they seep into the mind stream of human beings, encounter human habits, traditions, taboos, hang-ups, assumptions and principles. So, these also influence the form that the different methods take. ^383

In Tibet, for example, there are institutions like those of the Karmapa[39] or Ganden Tripa[40], where people would raise their eyebrows if the lineage holder suddenly had a girlfriend or got married. That would not be openly accepted by the mainstream at the time, even if in retrospect, some people romantically justify it by saying the lineage holder was a mahasiddha and so on. ^384

Actually, whether you are a monk or not is totally an individual choice. The Buddha’s path is never decided by caste, creed, marital status or job description. It is an individual path. And although an individual’s path should not be decided by tradition or culture, it does get decided by human constraints. I’m not saying that is good or bad. It’s just how the human mind works and manifests. ^385

The lineage to which I belong to doesn’t have a specific expectation that I should become a monk or a yogi ⎯ not at all. Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo[41], for instance, never outwardly had a consort[42], but Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro[43] had one. "Consort", by the way, is tantric language. ^386

When I was growing up, there were people who, for many different reasons, always expected me to become fully ordained, especially when I was younger. Even now there are some who still expect this, though certainly fewer. Personally, I never had a strong desire to be a monk, for many reasons, including that I know my own character. Also, in my present situation in the modern world, I am constantly surrounded by people, which brings lots of emotions in both directions, so it is very difficult to be a bhikshu[44] in such circumstances. ^387

It’s fine to be bhikshu if you are just a simple monk living in the forest. You are nobody, and nobody comes to see you, so the chances of becoming a good bhikshu are much higher. But when you have a certain lineage name, plus all this hype going on, you are put in a situation where you are designed by society to attract a lot of people. And such attraction is of many kinds that are not necessarily only devotion. Being more and more aware of that and of my own character, I felt it is not so good to be hypocritical, especially because I think the vinaya is now already so murky in every tradition. So that’s why I have never been big on becoming a bhikshu, although I must say I have thought about it many times. ^388

Similarly, there has been a big group of people ⎯ maybe more than the first group ⎯ who, because I am the eldest son of the eldest son of the Dudjom lineage, have always had high expectations that I would get married and produce an heir. Now, in my very twisted mind, being a monk and being a father present similar issues ⎯ individually for me and for my character. Am I going to be a good father, a good husband, a good family person? Judging from my own character, I have to say I don’t think so. ^389

You see, whoever is my wife would suffer a lot. I tell all the girls not to think that lamas and Rinpoches are so attractive, and not to fall into that trap, because it’s a miserable life. ^390

64. But why do you have a girlfriend? ^391

I am not a saint who has conquered the need for security, companionship and fun. But if you ask me whether I am a good boyfriend, I don’t think so, and I would definitely not make a good husband at this moment. ^392

When people ask me why I have a girlfriend, I have a feeling they have a high expectation of me ⎯ that I am above a normal human being. This actually worries me. I would really like people to think that I am a hundred percent human, with all the human traits, weakness, strengths and flaws. When people’s expectations surpass the normal expectations they have of other human beings, it gets very difficult because people then expect me to do something beyond human behaviour while I am human. ^393

This all gets more complicated, because many times I do actually have to pretend that I am a little above the human for the sake of encouraging, leading and communicating with people. At times this backfires on me. In the ideal world, I would be happy if people follow me as a normal human being, instead of a super human being.  It is better if people see me as an ordinary human being who is trying hard to practice and appreciate the Buddhadharma, and who has the wish to help others understand the Buddhadharma. ^394

So as a normal human being, you get lonely. Ordinary people may not understand this, but I think I am talking about many other tulkus[45] as well as myself in this situation. People may not believe we get lonely, because they think that we are surrounded by hundreds who are ready to drop everything to do whatever we want. But that does not necessarily mean that tulkus and high Rinpoches never get lonely just because they have all those entertainments and such a busy schedule. ^395

So probably out of insecurity and loneliness, there were times that I courted girls to be with them. But there are also times that girls court me, which is a totally human thing to do. Karma also plays a very important part in this, because when the wind of karma blows in certain ways, people get together. No matter what you do to try to alter this, at times it is difficult to change. The only thing that I do is to aspire that , no matter where I get blown, pushed or pulled , one way or another I can benefit myself and the others. ^396

65. What is your attitude when you are in love? ^397

When I am in love, there are two sides: there’s the head and there’s the heart. In the head, there is all this information that nothing works, especially relationships, and especially when two egos meet. Just one ego and its own manifestations and activities have a hard enough time succeeding in any endeavour. When two egos meet, when two insecurities meet, then of course there’s no way for them to succeed. And then, people like me have heard and read about impermanence, and the game of emotion, and insecurity, so with my head, there is caution, and at least sometimes I won’t accept things just because of appearance or because of how I felt at that moment. ^398

I have to say that from a dharmic point of view, this information is a blessing because , even though I am not a good practitioner or practicing what I know to be the path , at least there is information in my head. When you're in love, you become blind, you don’t see these things, and your rationality disappears. Of course, when you meet the right person under the right conditions, then to a certain extent and for a certain amount of time, things may work. But this information can also make you skeptical, if you want to put it negatively, and if you want to put it positively, then this kind of information will keep you a little sober. ^399

Of course, there are times when the heart wins, or it may win for a few days or months. So I think this balancing of the head and the heart is something that even ordinary people should have, as it can only be good for them. ^400

I myself have seen so many people who, when they have relationship problems, just completely have no head, and are completely heart. And then I feel so sorry for them. They are just completely blind. But I have also seen people who are completely negative and pessimistic, and who are so afraid of falling into the heart, because they are so intellectual, logical, and afraid to fall in love. That’s also not good. But I have to point out the battle between heart and head goes on. ^401

66. I think most Chinese think that, in order to pursue a spiritual path, you are supposed to give up these things like ordinary love and relationship. So, why look for life in them? ^402

First of all, I’d like to say that there are a lot of things I’m supposed to give up that I have no strength to give up, and there are a lot of things I should do but am too weak to do. So based on my own experience as an individual, I think many people’s confusion on this issue is well-founded. ^403

But strictly speaking, from the dharmic point of view, you should give up whatever that feeds your pride, ego and greed. That could be relationship, but it could also be pretending to give up relationship. ^404

67. Why do you wear robes then? ^405

You're right. I think people might find it a little confusing looking at me wearing robes that, to the untrained eye, are very similar to those of monks. Most of what I am wearing is not really an ordained monk's robes, but it's unfair to expect most people to differentiate this. ^406

I should have kept my hair long in the tradition of lay practitioner yogis, but I don’t have much hair to keep. And this is loudly advertised by one of my students, Jing Rui, who calls me "Baldy"! ^407

But there is also something I need to clarify. There is a tradition in Tibetan Buddhism to wear robes even though you have a partner. Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro did so, and Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche as well. Now, I am in no way saying that I have the right to emulate them. In Tibetan we have a saying, "where the lion leaps, the fox should not attempt to jump", because otherwise it will break its ribs. ^408

But, as the previous Karma Chagme Rinpoche[46] said, this is the age of symbol, which is why we have this tradition of wearing robes. The belief is that there are four stages of the vinaya after the Buddha's parinirvana. After the age of dispute is the age of symbol; and the symbol of the Buddha, which is the monk's robe, is supposedly really inspiring to people. So this is why Karma Chagme Rinpoche and many Nyingma and Kagyu masters of the past and present follow this practice. I am also an admirer of this practice, because if we lose even the monk's robe, there will be nothing left. ^409

However, one thing I have to say is that I never know how things will change. I could become a monk any day, any time. But I could also get married. That can also happen. ^410

68. Some people with worldly achievements seem to have even stronger ego-clinging than ordinary people. Does that mean that such successful people will have more obstacles along the path towards liberation? ^411

Stronger ego-clinging means much more insecurity, much more paranoia, being more worked-up, blinder, and much less relaxed. So it’s just that people with stronger ego-clinging have a much thicker shell, which of course is more unfortunate. ^412

People like Jigme Lingpa have prayers after prayers that never to be reborn as Bill Gates, because when you are worldly successful, the tendency of not being able to practice is much more. But that doesn’t mean that all successful people are the ones with more ego-clinging or more obstacles on the path. Often in fact, many of these so-called rich, well-to-do and successful people — if they have merit — are the ones who turn to a spiritual path when they realize that all their money and worldly success have not bought them happiness. The Buddha himself had everything from the worldly point of view, and due to his extraordinary merit, he matured on the spiritual path within a short time. ^413

It also does not mean that all poor people have much better chances to practice the dharma. Often people who are poor from the beginning are not necessarily suited for the spiritual path, because they still haven’t tasted worldly success all the way. Many times they entangle themselves by thinking they are poor and they have to become rich. ^414

69. Do you have any suggestion for what we can do when we meet someone successful with a really puffed-up ego? ^415

If you have access to these people, and if you are skilful, you can definitely try to reach them through all sorts of means. Preaching at them might not work. But, as I said earlier, those with a lot of ego actually have a lot of insecurity. Where there is a lot of smoke, there is a lot of fire. So where there is insecurity ⎯ if you learn what kind of insecurity a person has ⎯ you can tap into that and communicate through that very insecurity. ^416

But there is a reason why great masters like Jigme Lingpa never wanted to reborn as a worldly successful egomaniac ⎯ because firstly they don’t listen, and even if they do listen they don’t hear, because they are so swollen with ego. ^417

But there is still no reason to give up on them. If you are friends with such people and if you care for them, you can practice and dedicate the merit to them so that, one way or another, such people will be connected to the dharma, if not this life, then maybe after ten lifetimes. For Mahayana practitioners, after all, it is never too late. ^418

70. Why does ego-clinging sometimes appear even more strongly for practitioners? Does that mean that when we are touching or trying to crush our ego through practice, it rebels? Is this a valid question? ^419

Yes, very valid. This is one of the most common problems. What it means is the dharma has gone inside the head, not the heart. The dharma has become yet another tool of ego. Sometimes people who have worn out many malas and crushed many cushions may appear to be good practitioners, but it’s only appearance. They may have shut themselves into a room pretending to practice, but are not really practicing. That is very common. ^420

My teachers told me that there are two signs of being learned and being a good practitioner. The best sign of being learned is you become humble. The best sign of being a good practitioner is that you have less emotion ⎯ you don't get worked up. If you don't have these two signs, you are not learned and you haven’t practiced. ^421

I know a khenpo who is very learned and well versed in the texts and scriptures, keeps his vows diligently, and is so proud of his knowledge and discipline. Being born in Tibet, he is also male chauvinist and tends to look down at women. Sometimes it is difficult to differentiate whether is he looking down on women because of the Tibetan culture or because he does so on the pretext of his vinaya practice, which is what he always used to boast. ^422

In the same temple, I remember a local nun who was not well-known or learned, but who cleaned the offering bowls in the temple very diligently and prepared the butter lamp offerings. These are tedious tasks in monasteries which not many people want to do. This khenpo was not respectful to that nun. ^423

Anyway, as years went by, the nun developed a health problem and died. She died so gracefully that I remembered the verses from the sutras in which Sakyamuni Buddha said:“Genuine followers of mine will die like a lion”. I remembered those verses very strongly as I looked at the way she died, with no possessions, with nothing to protect, and so relaxed. ^424

And then, sometime later, the khenpo had a car accident and broke his neck. He did not die but was close to death. I learned a lot by how he manifested. He had so much fear because the dharma never went inside his heart. He was in a panic, acting as any ordinary person would who does not wish to die yet feels death coming and doesn’t know what to do with it. ^425

All the knowledge this khenpo had about impermanence and death was just intellectual. Even more embarrassing was that he tried to hide his fear at that time, because even in this dire situation, he was still  vain and wanted to pretend he did not care, even though he actually cared a lot. ^426

This actually frightened me a lot. When I saw that, I thought, what if I end up like this khenpo? So ego-clinging appears in the most unlikely person’s heart. ^427

But as you say in your question, it is also possible that when we practice, some practitioners’ egos initially appear to be stronger, because their egos are being fished out and therefore rebelling. In such cases, it’s actually not that bad. ^428

Chapter 6: About Different Vehicles ^429

71. For people who have not studied Buddhism intensively, how should they understand the differences between the different yanas[47] and why the Buddha taught 84,000 methods? How can we choose a paththat suits us, and what is so special about Tibetan Buddhism? ^430

In 2009, I got sick in Australia, and a doctor eventually had to remove my gallbladder. Prior to that, there was a lot of argument among my friends and students, out of their love and care for me. One group was totally against western methods of surgery and proposed traditional medicines and treatments like Chinese acupuncture, homeopathy, naturopathy and so on. Anyway, in the end, because I had such severe pain, it became an emergency situation and I had no choice but to rely on western allopathic treatment and to have surgery. ^431

The Buddhadharma is like those different kinds of medicine and treatment, each of which works for different people in different situations. In fact, 84,000 methods is just a symbolic expression, and there are actually many more methods than that, because there are so many defilements, emotions, sicknesses and internal afflictions, which all require different treatments. ^432

So for different beings who have different ailments, different weaknesses and different cultures, the Buddha gave different teachings. Tibetan Buddhism is no more special than the others, and it is very arrogant for Tibetans to think that their particular methods are the most special. ^433

Teachers, in order to encourage students and prevent them getting distracted, tend to say that one particular method is the best for you. But that is just a way of training the mind and should not be understood as implying that one method is better than the other. ^434

The Buddha himself said in the Prajnaparamita Sutra that if somebody thinks that a certain teaching is made for Shravakayana people, Mahayana people or Pratyekayana[48] people, that is not a good attitude. Segregating and putting preferences on the Buddha’s teachings is disrespectful, because every single word of the Buddha is so special and precious. Chinese Mahayana Buddhism should never look down on Shravakayana and Theravada Buddhism[49] as being lower, and Tibetan Buddhism should never look down on other paths. ^435

I am not trying to promote political correctness here, but rather to promote the wisdom of knowing how precious the Buddha’s teachings are. Each teaching is meant for a different audience, capacity and time. For example, when the Buddha taught the monks, he might tell them not to bring girls into their proximity. But that has nothing to do despising women, because in other teachings, like the tantric instructions, women are the essence of wisdom. ^436

Another example is that the Buddha taught the monks that, when begging alms, they must accept whatever is offered. In that context, you may see Theravada monks accepting meat. Mahayana practitioners should not criticize Theravada monks for that. In fact, the Buddha is not giving permission for practitioners to eat meat, but is simply saying monks should not choose when they beg. And in Vajrayana, the Buddha’s advice that you should not choose is interpreted from a very different angle , namely that you should not choose, because choosing is making a judgement and expressing a preference, which is a dualistic thought. ^437

So the different vehicles of Buddhism alone demonstrate the greatness of the Buddha. He was not teaching to one type of people, and his teaching is not narrowed by culture, time or situation. If it were limited in this way, then all followers of the Buddha should be wearing dhotis and be bare-chested, because that was common when he taught in India2,500 years ago. ^438

But at the same time, one should not think that, in teaching different methods to suit different disciples, the Buddha was simply entertaining people by telling them whatever they want to hear. The ultimate aim of all the Buddha’s instructions is to crush the dualistic ego. In fact, there is not a single teaching of the Buddha that encourages us to keep our dualistic mind. ^439

This alone proves the Buddha’s limitless compassion and limitless capacity. So a wise seeker of a genuine spiritual path will realize that the endless choices available for one’s own spiritual growth are such good news. ^440

72. How do we choose a right path? ^441

I am not the best person to answer how to choose a path, because, in a way, I am so fortunate to have been born in a Buddhist country and into a Buddhist family. Basically, as soon as I stumbled out of my mother’s womb, everything was Buddhist ⎯ the shrine, the prayer flags, the stupa ⎯ everything in fact. ^442

On the other hand, the process of seeking and searching for a spiritual path is probably one of the most important elements of the spiritual journey, and this is something I never had. Yes, I had this later, but I was already a Buddhist, though still a searching kind of Buddhist. I was so inspired by an Iranian I know, who lives with his Muslim family in a Muslim country, and searched for a spiritual path that finally led him to Buddhism. ^443

Satisfaction, ironically, comes when you are not really finding, but you are still searching. The whole process of searching is so maturing, and so good and rewarding, if only people can be quite persistent in their searching. ^444

Many people search, but get distracted along the way by material things. There are, as we discussed earlier, 84,000 paths and methods from which to choose. You can choose whatever you feel inclined towards, and you could meet this spiritual path in so many ways ⎯ by reading a book or by dating a girl who wears a Buddhist amulet. ^445

While you are searching, as Patrul Rinpoche[50] said, you should be like a hungry yak, relentlessly diligent and greedy, but it is also very important to be really open-minded and critically-minded. One must be critical of the critical mind as well. So, from being stupid, you have to get smart, and eventually you have to get rid of being smart also. ^446

73. What kind of faculties of sentient beings are suited to Thesravakayana,  Bodhisattvayana[51] and Vajrayana paths? ^447

Broadly, we are talking about beings’ capacities, which can be many things. Basically, the main thing is the capacity to appreciate, chew and swallow the view. The majority of us don’t have the capacity to hear, listen, contemplate and appreciate when someone says, “there is no such thing as samsara and nirvana”, because we will end up hearing a very nihilistic view. ^448

It really depends on what kind of propensity and faculties you have been brought up with. Let’s say you grew up in a family that loves God, and all they talked about is God’s existence, blessings, power, omniscience, omnipotence, and all that. Sometimes when there is too much of that, you will feel like revolting against that view. You want to hear something else. That is how the human mind works. Sometimes that kind of mentality may be a perfect vessel to hear about emptiness. ^449

But at the same time, you could be brought up in a very eternalistic, theistic family, and then you yourself believe in God and all this existence very strongly. That needs to be really destroyed. That rug needs to be pulled out from under your feet, almost in a violent way. With the right condition and right situation, that can also be done. All these details, only the Buddha knows. ^450

These differing propensities can even differ between morning and evening within the same person’s psyche. In the morning you may be more inclined to emptiness, and in the evening you may be more inclined to existence. Now what method should be applied? Should the teacher go along with these changing inclinations or against them? That really depends so much on the skill and compassion of the teacher. When Manjushri spoke about shunyata, it is believed that 500 arhats[52] died of heart attacks. I am sure Manjushri knew what the consequences of his teaching would be, but probably that was exactly what was necessary. The fact that these arhats vomited blood means that they had some understanding. ^451

Take the Vajrayana for instance, which has different methods categorized in different Tantras. Some Vajrayana teachings, such as the Kriya Tantra[53], teach us discipline, such as being vegetarian, cleanliness, and an approach to the deity and mandala[54] that promotes distinctions. This kind of tantric teaching can suit a mind that is influenced by Confucian values and that has a strong sense of order and a code of conduct. ^452

By contrast, the teaching of Anuyoga[55] involves much more daring methods that do not promote distinctions, and its practices therefore even allow use of normally guarded and circumspect substances like wine and meat. It might also work for a skilful master to teach Anuyoga to similar kinds of people who have lots of distinctions. ^453

So it is said in the Tantra that if an unskilled and cowardly teacher insists on giving more general teachings that have more distinctions, like not eating meat and being clean, to a student with vast capacity, the teacher is wasting that student’s life, which is really considered very bad. ^454

It works the other way round too. If a tantric teacher unskilfully displays a tantric deity embracing a consort to a very narrow, ethical, moralistic and righteous student who, by seeing this photo, completely loses faith in the Vajrayana, then the teacher has destroyed that student’s potential to become a tantric practitioner, which is a very grave mistake. ^455

74. What are the major differences inthe precepts observed by practitioners of Shravakayana, Bodhisattvayana and Vajrayana? ^456

Because of the different capacities of different people, the view is introduced differently. It is ultimately the same view  just the dosage is different. People of lesser capacity will have a much more defined code of conduct such as that one should not kill, lie or steal; whereas people following Mahayana teachings, who supposedly have greater capacity, are given a greater view with a much broader definition. That makes the bodhisattva path much more complicated, and at the same time very rewarding, because not killing, lying or stealing can be breaking the vow if those actions don’t benefit all sentient beings. ^457

For people of lesser capacity, the solution and the problem are very clearly defined and differentiated; whereas for people with greater capacity, the difference between solution and problem ultimately does not exist. Where Tantra is taught, the problem itself is the solution. ^458

So broadly speaking, a key difference between the precepts taught in the different vehicles is that what is prohibited in Shravakayana may be allowed in Mahayana, and what is prohibited in the Mahayana may be allowed in Tantrayana. ^459

75. What makes Vajrayana a much quicker path to enlightenment than paths in other yanas? ^460

“Quick” and “slow” are from a practitioner’s point of view, and are only relative notions. But as a method, Vajrayana is indeed quicker. Let’s say you are thirsty, and we know that your thirst needs to be quenched. But we have a bigger problem, because you don’t believe that this water is actually water. So we have to trick you to make you drink, saying that the water is milk, orange juice, honey or something else. We colour the water to make it more attractive, and then we can make you drink. That takes time, because you have to squeeze the lemon, colour and label the water differently, persuade you it’s good for you, and more. But if you have the capacity to look at the water and accept it as water directly, then the job is done and you just drink it. ^461

It is important to know that when we talk about Vajrayana being the quick path, we are purely talking from the path point of view. But in actual fact, how long it takes really depends on the one who is dwelling on the path.  Someone could be very cautious and take a long but safe route. Another person could take a shortcut, but that might have many obstacles. So if you don’t know how to deal with these obstacles, the person taking the long route might reach the point quicker than the one supposedly taking the shortcut. ^462

76. In Vajrayana, is keeping samaya[56] something achievable for us? ^463

It is very difficult. The moment you think, you are breaking samaya. But the great thing about Vajrayana is also that the moment you think, you can also mend samaya. So it is a very, very, very beautiful path. Mending samaya has more chance to be ultimately successful than breaking samaya, because when you are mending, you are getting closer to the truth, and the truth never fails. So this is what makes Vajrayana very special. ^464

For instance, if you have taken a vow not to eat after noon and then you happen to eat something, that’s it. You cannot sort of vomit it out and say you did not eat it. Whereas in Vajrayana, if you think someone is beautiful and someone else is ugly, you are breaking the vow. But you can immediately mend it by chanting mantras, applying the view, and correcting the thought immediately. Also, there is method in which just eating a biscuit with a certain view accumulates more merit than inviting thousands of Buddhas for lunch. So it’s crazy not to practice Vajrayana. ^465

77. What should we do if we encounter people who misunderstand Vajrayana? ^466

In the ideal world, one should never make Vajrayana available to those who do not appreciate it. A person who is open-minded and searching for a path, and who encounters but misinterprets Vajrayana, can be corrected through hearing, contemplation and meditation. ^467

But of course, there are beings who oppose Vajrayana, because they really misunderstand it and see it as evil or contaminated. For these beings, if there is time, openness and the right situation, one should try to present the logic and reasoning that establish the Vajrayana view, meditation methods and so on. But if someone is not interested in hearing any of these, the best way is to avoid that person and pray for his or her enlightenment from a distance. ^468

78. There are fourteen samayas[57] in the Vajrayana practice, the transgression of which can cause practitioners to fall into vajrahell. Does this mean that the practice of Vajrayana is not very secure, and what can be done if we break these samayas? ^469

First of all, it is important to understand that there is no such thing as first trying to keep all the samayas and then practicing, because keeping the samayas is the practice. It is said in the Mahayana sutras that all other vows are like a clay pot that cannot really be mended when it breaks, whereas the Mahayana vows are like a golden pot that, with the right goldsmith, can be made even more beautiful if it gets dented. ^470

Similarly, it may be easy to break the Vajrayana samayas, but it is equally easy to mend them. And when we talk about going to vajra hell after breaking the root samayas, I’d like to point out that vajra hell is not necessarily some hideous place reserved for samaya breakers. Vajrahell is basically the state where one cannot appreciate and conceive the absolute non-duality. You may be righteous, law-abiding and morally sound, but if you don’t have the capacity to understand non-duality in the ultimate sense, then for Vajrayana practitioner, this is vajrahell, because it looks good and so you may not wish to get out of it. ^471

79. Many Chinese students like to recite or even transcribe the Vajracchedika Prajnaparamita Sutra or the Heart Sutra. In the Vajracchedika Prajnaparamita Sutra, Buddha said that you cannot see the tathagata if you are seeking him as having a“real” form or voice. However, Vajrayana provides many practices based on form, such as visualizations, recitations, prostrations and mantras, etc. What is the right way to understand these approaches? ^472

In the Vajracchedika Sutra, the Buddha said that those who see the Buddha as a form have a wrong view. And this is exactly what Vajrayana is also trying to express, but in a totally opposite way. After all, the whole point of prajnaparamita[58] is that if you have a dualistic distinction about the Buddha, such as that he has a form, then form implies inches, centimetres, feet, weight, and then of course colour and shape. But if you see the Buddha within the limitation of colour, shape and size, then you are not understanding the Buddha. This point is skilfully taught in Vajrayana. ^473

In Vajrayana, you might visualize the Buddha as a deity with a thousand arms sitting on a lotus, which physically is impossible because the lotus will be crushed. Having more than two arms, three eyes and extra heads breaks ourconcept of human form; and when the Buddha comes with an animal head, that really breaks down the whole concept of form. So in such ways, the tantric teachings on deity and mandala complement exactly what the Vajracchedika Sutra teaches. ^474

80. There are many examples in both the Zen and Mahasandhi traditions of practitioners attaining enlightenment. Could you talk about the similarities and differences between Zen and Atiyoga[59] practices? ^475

I am not qualified to express the differences between these two paths. First, I never really studied Zen and my Vajrayana understanding is limited. Very generically, Zen Buddhism seems to be the quintessence of the Mahayana path. Zen Buddhism uses Mahayana sutras such as the Prajnaparamita Sutra. So that is their ground and fundamental base. ^476

Mahasandhi is a Vajrayana teaching that not only uses all the Mahayana sutras that I just mentioned, but also uses the tantric methods. I feel that Zen and Mahasandhiare the same as far as the view is concern, and some of their meditation techniques are also identical, but there may be differences when it comes to the methods they use. For example, Mahasandhi will never reject rituals, whereas Zen might deliberately refrain from that. In one way, this may be a small difference, but on the other hand, it is a big difference, because it shows the daringness of Mahasandhi. I think that Zen Buddhism is so special and profound and I really hope China will uphold those teachings and practices. ^477

81. In Vajrayana, what is the viewpoint towards Sukhavāti-related practices? Does Vajrayana also provide some practices for people who have aspired to go there? ^478

In many ways, the pure land practices are so close to Vajrayana, because in Vajrayana we also talk about pure land and pure perception[60]. Maybe there are only a few differences, such as that in pure land practices, the achievement of the pure land is almost physical ⎯ that you practice now with the aspiration to be reborn in the pure realm. By contrast, in Vajrayana,it is more like the realization and discovery of the pure land, which is right here, where you are right now reading this book. ^479

82. Do you have any suggestions to pure land school disciples on whether they will be cable to go to the pure land more swiftly if they combine the pure land and Vajrayana practices together? ^480

Practitioners don’t need to have both these practices in order to go swiftly. But if they have both, it is a bit like carrying an extra battery. ^481

Practicing the pure land path is a practice of devotion and trust, and those who wish to go to the pure land must generate that trust. To do that, one must generate doubt to our conventional doubt. So when we face towards the west and open our eyes, we don’t see the Amitabha Buddha realm at the moment. That’s because our minds are accustomed to thinking that the Amitabha realm cannot be there because we cannot see it, or that we have never received a letter from the Amitabha realm describing how it is. ^482

But if we look and contemplate carefully, we will realize that all these doubts we have are based on very petty, superficial reasons, because we dare not think beyond our usual way of thinking. So in this way, we should learn to doubt the way we usually think and the way we usually come to conclusions. Then we can gradually generate one-pointed, unwavering confidence that the land of Amitabha is there. When we achieve that kind of unwavering conviction, we have received the blessing of Amitabha. ^483

83. Since there are also many pure land rituals in the Vajrayana practice, what should Vajrayana practitioners target ⎯ enlightenment in this life, or being reborn in a pureland and starting from there? ^484

Generally in Vajrayana, being reborn in a pureland is enlightenment. That is how Vajrayana interprets it. ^485

84. But we should aspire to let others go first? ^486

Yes, of course. That is the fundamental bodhisattva attitude. ^487

85. A friend of mine thinks he is practicing non-duality, and therefore that longing for Sukhavati[61] is an attachment that should be avoided. Another friend thinks the pure land school is good for everyone and that there is no reason or need for other paths even to exist. Rinpoche, do you think these two views conflict with each other, and is it possible for a practitioner to absorb the quintessence of both and utilize them in daily practice? ^488

They are both wrong. The first friend never understood that the pureland is the non-duality land. The second friend is depriving Buddhism of its abundance of methods. Choice is always good to have, so why should one deprive oneself of choice? ^489

86. Could you talk about different faculties and their attributes? Many students are very curious about what their particular faculty is. Is this important to know? Is it safe to say that basically nobody has the supreme faculty, except those who came into this world through the strength of their aspirations? ^490

This is not that important, and it’s not necessary for people really to know what kind of faculty they have. But what is important is to have the aspiration to have the best faculty. ^491

87. Do we have to have the right faculty to practice Vajrayana? ^492

Yes, one has to have the right faculty to practice Vajrayana, and fundamentally the right faculty to practice Vajrayana is openness and devotion towards that path. ^493

88. What if people ask you, Rinpoche, whether they have the right faculty to practice Vajrayana? ^494

I personally cannot answer that, because I am an ignorant being. Nevertheless, as I said, the main faculties for the Vajrayana practice are openness and devotion. You may think you have devotion towards the guru and the triple gem, revulsion towards samsara and so on. But to go against some of our old habits and really to shrug off some of the old inhibitions is tough. ^495

As well, to accept some of the mind boggling stuff of Vajrayana as path is very tough. If a lama says you should not steal, you cannot argue with that. Such advice is expected from a spiritual master, but the opposite is not expected. So if the lama tells you to steal, then it is not only difficult to do so without any doubt, but you will even begin to doubt the master. ^496

89. Could Rinpoche talk about pure perception and the reason why it is so important in Vajrayana. ^497

The word “pure” is important. It should not be misunderstood as choosing “pure” as opposed to “impure”. “Pure” here is going beyond pure and impure. When we speak of having pure perception of the guru, and if you perceive your guru as having a parrot nose, it does not mean that you have to correct that into a human nose in order to purify your perception. Rather, it means to think that parrot nose, human nose or heavenly nose are all just my perception, and that there is no externally and truly existing parrot or human nose. That is a good beginning and approach to pure or right perception. ^498

90. Why is it only in Vajrayana that we find the view of pure perception? Do we find it in other schools? ^499

That’s because Vajrayana is supposedly a path that uses the result as the path. The result is pure even in Mahayana, so we are already thinking that then. It is like washing dishes, where you already know, accept and visualize that they are going to be clean. ^500

Actually pure perception is a more advanced and sophisticated way of expressing the compassion that is taught in Mahayana. In Mahayana, all sentient beings are seen as your mothers, and so they deserve to be taken care of. Some of them may appear as friends and some as enemies, but in reality the Mahayana path requests us to understand them equally as mother sentient beings. That is already pure perception, even though it is not called that way. ^501

Vajrayana takes this a step further. Sentient beings are not just our mothers but are deities. When you think of all sentient beings as our mothers, you are basically doing this to generate compassion ⎯ assuming that these beings who have been our mothers in our past lives are ignorant and imprisoned by their karma and emotions, and that therefore it is our duty to liberate them. ^502

In Vajrayana, on top of that, we look at the absolute nature of sentient beings, which is their Buddha Nature. So they are not just ignorant, pathetic, helpless mothers. Their nature is Buddha. Because Vajrayana uses the result as the path[62], and since Buddhahood is the result, so you already see the result in sentient beings. It’s just like the earlier example of already seeing the clean dish when you wash the dish, especially if you are a confident dishwasher. ^503

91. Why are there different schools even in Vajrayana? As the reincarnation of one of the best gurus in the Rimé[63] tradition, could you talk about the similarities and differences between these schools, and do you have any comment on the meaning of non-sectarianism? ^504

We should label different medicines for different ailments, recognizing that this is a medicine for headache, this is a medicine for toothache, and this is a medicine for nose-ache, rather than saying that this is a medicine for black people and this is a medicine for white people. ^505

Unfortunately, when the Mahayana or Vajrayana people categorize Buddha’s teachings, there tends to be some elitism. When people say, “oh, that’s the Shravakayana path”, there is always a slight looking down on it. That actually creates a lot of bad karma, because they are looking down on the very teaching that is coming out of the same mouth from which their own teaching came. ^506

So being sectarian is actually one of the most poisonous traits. Sectarianism is also one of the most negative human habits and is going to be the biggest downfalls and weaknesses among Buddhists in the years to come. ^507

Defeating the sectarian problem is going to be difficult, but I think that it’s at least good to talk about why it’s a problem. The sectarian problem is so deeply rooted especially among people who are not too culturally advanced. It gives them comfort to be sectarian because people like belonging to a club and to a party. ^508

But I still have a hope that non-sectarianism will prevail in this day and age of critical thinking when people are more intellectually oriented. Hopefully in places like China, the Chinese will be smart enough to discard some of the bad Tibetan sectarian habits. I am afraid that Tibetans will deliberately teach a sectarian approach because they think it is the right thing to do, but I am totally against it. ^509

92. For lay Buddhists, it is hard to follow more than one lineage. How can we stick with one and still be non-sectarian? ^510

That is very easy. It is like when you have a headache, you should take headache pills. It does not mean that you should condemn or disrespect other medicines, or promote headache pills for those who have stomachache or footache. Likewise, for you, a certain lineage is inspiring, beneficial and effective, and you should enjoy that. You should think that just as this particular method is working for you, methods in certain other lineages must be working for others. ^511

93. Is there a way to combine together all the advantages of different schools to guide our study and practice? Or there is no need to do that at all? ^512

Actually, it is not necessary to combine them. The great thing about the Buddhist paths is that every path is complete. But it is also said that one great thing to do is to practice Shravakayana outwardly, Mahayana inwardly, and Vajrayana secretly. This is actually a very important pith instruction. ^513

Chapter 7: About Guru and Lineage ^514

94. What is lineage? What kind of lineage is regarded as immaculate and authentic? Why is an immaculate and authentic lineage critical to practitioners? ^515

This is a very important and big question. I am actually writing a book about this separately. There are books like The Power of Now that was written by an American. He seemed to claim that what he writes is a revelation that just came to him, but there are so many things in the book that one can see are extracted from the Buddhist teachings. Of course, the guy speaks in local idioms much better than do the traditional teachings, so it suits the local culture. ^516

In the west, especially in America, though probably not so much in Asia yet, people tend not to like organized religion. But in the process, a different kind of attitude has developed, in which they begin to dislike tradition and ancient heritages altogether. ^517

It is my own observation — and I could be totally wrong — that in the west, people have suffered under the church, because the church and people connected to the church have abused their power. That actually happens not only in the west, but also in the east, where temples, priests, monks, Rinpoches, abbots and others abuse their power. At least something good about the west is that people really speak up and go against the church and the church’s decisions if they are wrong. ^518

But this distrust of organized religion has helped produce this modern phenomenon in which, with a few different words and analogies, people are basically teaching the Buddha’s teachings and claiming them as their own. Then their audience and readers think:“Wow, how great and profound and brilliant this person is.” Such people don’t mention any institution or tradition from which they got the teachings, and say they are just a Californian businessman, for example. That’s less threatening and in fact more appealing to many modern people. ^519

But these kinds of people could be ruining the path, because there are now so many self-appointed spiritual guides appearing. This can be very, very dangerous because there is no way to judge who is genuine and who is not. I myself can’t judge, and can’t really say the author of The Power of Now is not a mahasiddha. He could be a mahasiddha, because only a mahasiddha can judge whether another is a mahasiddha or not. But we have to be careful, because this kind of manifestation can ruin the path. ^520

There is always this running debate between practitioners and intellectuals. A lot of students are told not to spend so much time just reading books and intellectualizing, but are strongly told just to practice. Practitioners say:“What is the point of cooking and arranging your food but never eating?” ^521

But on the other side, the intellectuals say that if you don’t study, it’s very dangerous. If you only follow your blind motivation and don’t have a clue what you are practicing, you are not only being naïve, but it can be very dangerous. For example, you can lose your devotion or confidence in the dharma unexpectedly, and then you have no rational mind to bring back your devotion. But if you have engaged in some analytical and critical studies, then even though your guru or your dharma brothers and sisters behave badly, you can always go back to that intellectual confidence: “Yes, my guru is behaving badly, my dharma brothers and sisters are behaving badly, but emptiness is good, dependent arising is the truth”, and so on. ^522

So organized religion and established institutions become very important as a check and balance against the tendency for anyone just to claim how great they are. Of course, they could be great, but maybe not, and who is going to judge? So the best and in fact the only thing we have is analysis based on some established organization and institution. We can’t get rid of that, which is why institution or lineage becomes such an important factor. ^523

Then also, we are victims of causes and conditions. No matter what, this dependence on causes and conditions is something we cannot escape. Guru and lineage happen to be one of the most important causes and conditions for our spiritual journey. This is why ⎯ not just in Vajrayana but in all the vehicles ⎯ the master is so important. In Vajrayana, the guru is the path, so not wanting a guru is a contradiction. ^524

95. Why is the guru so important in the practice of Vajrayana? How do we understand that the guru is all Three Jewels, and on the most profound level that the guru is the entire spiritual path? ^525

Because everything is our mind’s projection. The prostitute down the street, the gangsters in the White House, the thief in Zhongnanhai, the mafia in the Kremlin and the guru above your head are all the projections of your mind. The Kremlin gangster might only inspire you to slurp Russian goulash soup; the American White House gangster might inspire you to smoke a cigar; while the prostitute might make you lose your appetite. ^526

Among all the projections of your mind, the guru is the one figure that your karma, your merit and you yourself decided and concluded that this man or woman inspires you to think about the truth ⎯ especially the truth of the nature of mind. This is why the guru really becomes so important, especially in Vajrayana. ^527

96. Why it is so seldom that we see an enlightened sublime being around us? How do we convince ourselves that to get enlightened is possible? ^528

This is why the true bodhisattva’s aspiration to dwell, roam around and live long on this earth is so important. But at the end of the day, you have to have the merit actually to recognize the sublime being when he or she comes to your door step. If you really have amazing merit, you will see the sublime being when he is in front of you. ^529

97. How do we accumulate such merit? ^530

The longing ⎯ the longing to meet and be with an enlightened being, and the longing to extract the qualities and blessings from that enlightened being ⎯ is always the key. Once you have that longing and strive for that, then you will not see challenges as pain, and everything you do will be a means to accumulate merit. ^531

Fundamentally, the wish not to dwell in the worldly samsara and to pursue the path of enlightenment not just for oneself but for all sentient beings, and then with that motivation as a base, to remember the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha, to begin to discipline oneself, to be generous to others, to be diligent in hearing, contemplating and meditating, and to generate wisdom⎯all these will increase your merit. ^532

Q: How do we differentiate longing from attachment and grasping? ^533

Initially you cannot, but that is fine. ^534

In fact, infatuation, having a crush, feeling comfortable with a certain person, feeling that you can understand that person or that he can understand you, and even mundane things such as that he likes the food you like, you like the music that he likes to listen to⎯many of these  very mundane things can be an igniter. Since we are mundane people, we have to begin somewhere. ^535

You could go to a guru because you like the way he looks or dresses, and with that as kindling, all the spiritual devotion and path can begin. Of course, one has to be careful that this mundane connection will not hijack or take over the spiritual connection. ^536

98. There are so many sentient beings to liberate, while great gurus are so rare. What can we do? Will the situation improve or get even worse in the future? ^537

As the Buddha said, as long as there are sentient beings, there will be Buddhas. As we see so many sentient beings, we should be confident and relaxed and rest assured that there are thousands more Buddhas and bodhisattvas working so hard to liberate them. ^538

99. Could Rinpoche talk about different types of vajra gurus? ^539

There are kind gurus, learned gurus, disciplined gurus, gurus with all three of these qualities, as well as hidden gurus, pompous gurus, gurus with lots of activities, traditional institutionalized gurus and more. ^540

But from a strictly spiritual point of view, the guru is an absolutely individual thing. You have to find your guru, who has nothing to do with education, rank, colour or shape, but is someone who gives you the path to enlightenment, who is concerned about your enlightenment, and who is courageous enough to tell you what you need to hear, not just what you want to hear. ^541

100. Some gurus manifest as crazy yogis with no attachment to anything. How can we tell the manifestations from a real guru from ordinary crazy behaviour? ^542

That is very difficult, so difficult. We have the responsibility to test these gurus. Even though there’s no perfect test, you can use some common sense. If a guru behaves crazily and does something outrageous, you could probably try being crazy and outrageous towards him. If that doesn’t really upset him or change his values, actions and way of thinking too much, then maybe he is not so bad. You could try using his own methods to test him. He could insult or ignore you, so you could do that towards him and see whether he gets upset or embarrassed. ^543

That takes a lot of courage, but before you accept such a personas a guru, it would be good if you can do such tests. But most people are too impatient and usually don’t do those things. They usually end up jumping rushing to conclude a samaya with one guru, and that is always a bit of a weakness. ^544

It takes a long time, years in fact. One really important thing is to observe how much this guru is interested in worldly life, and how much this guru is interested not only in his own spiritual values but also in the students’ spiritual values. ^545

There are a lot of gurus who behave politically correctly and are morally sound, but they may be totally paranoid about what people think. And if the gurus are too caught up with what people think, that means they are only interested in the picture, the façade, the appearance. So that’s a big deception. But it works because people are only interested in appearance. We hear this a lot from people:“Oh, my guru is so good, he always smiles a lot”. We never hear:“Oh, my guru is so good, he is always scolding and frowning”. ^546

I really admire Trungpa Rinpoche’s[64] students who are so devoted to him, because generally speaking, Trungpa Rinpoche wasn’t the most well-behaved person. These students, especially the western students, are not idiots. They are all smart, but not only were they so devoted to Trungpa Rinpoche when he was alive, but their devotion continues after he has passed away. To me this shows that this particular guru had something to offer, and these guys realized it. ^547

There are masters who are very kind and who look very nice as Buddhist monks, and they also have amazing disciples who behave well. But they are never tested, and the students’ devotion is also never tested. So I don’t know. Whether a guru is good or not, one can tell only when he or she is tested by the situation, and by causes and conditions. ^548

101. What is the right way to look at the crazy behaviour of those great gurus and to develop devotion for them ⎯ if not immediately then through gradual training? ^549

If the very motivation of looking for the guru and choosing that particular guru has begun with the aim of seeking enlightenment and understanding the truth, then all the rest will be easier and is not that important. But if you are seeking for your own self-gratification, for wanting to be part of a very important institution, or to be associated with an important guru, then you will begin to judge the guru because of his or her behaviour and appearance. So motivation is very important. ^550

So once you have the right motivation even in looking for the guru, then with this motivation as a base, one can apply methods of analysing the guru. If your own common sense convinces you that this is a great teacher, then unwavering devotion will grow slowly and gradually. ^551

Generally, it’s important to have a strong understanding of how everything is our projection and everything is our mind. For instance, when a boy falls for a girl, he will see her as the most beautiful, most appealing, most desirable object in the whole world, and that is a projection. Of course, when you don’t like the same person, you see different faults. So the boy, after being with this girl for many years, might begin to see faults. This proves that everything is just your projection. ^552

Having a firm understanding that everything is your projection can help maintain pure perception, because even the guru, like everything else, is your own projection. So when you see the guru as a perfect being, that  is your projection. When you begin to see the guru making mistakes, that is also your projection. So in having this awareness that everything is your perception, I would say that 85% of the pure perception practice is achieved. ^553

102. As your disciple, Rinpoche, no matter what you do, our confidence in you will not be affected. However, it is hard to maintain the same kind of pure perception towards different gurus. Could you tell us why? ^554

That may be just a karmic link, karmic debt or karmic connection, which is not necessarily a bad thing, especially if it is accompanied by right motivation and training. But of course, having one guru is not a reason to dislike other gurus. It also doesn’t mean that you have to follow every guru on earth, or that you can only have one guru. ^555

Sometimes Tibetan lamas seem to advocate that once you become a student of one guru, then you should only be following that guru. That is not necessarily true. It really depends on how much capacity you have. Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo had 113 gurus. Other lamas would advise not to roam around with too many gurus. That makes sense because sometimes students, especially very young students, can find it very confusing if they hear too many different instructions. ^556

As for my so-called students, I don’t know what they have ⎯ whether it is devotion, attachment, or just blind hallucination, but so far they haven’t seen me sleeping with Douglas Mills. If they see Douglas Mills dripping with sweat and sleeping with me, they might probably change their minds. ^557

103. For those yogis, are their crazy behaviours also their practice? Can we emulate them? ^558

Yes, such behaviour could be their practice, and it could be the manifestation of their compassion. They want to pull the rug out from under your feet constantly. They constantly want to break your concepts, and they also consistently want to break their own concepts. ^559

When you have confidence in your own right view, and when you are confident in your ability not to get affected by hope and fear, then yes, you should try to emulate them. ^560

But if you have even a little bit of hope and fear, then you should not emulate them, but rather you should aspire to become like them, because where the lion jumps, the fox cannot jump. ^561

104. Could Rinpoche talk about the concept of root guru? ^562

The definition of root guru is quite complex. The root guru sometimes refers to a person from whom you have received abhishekas. The concept of root guru is a very tantric thing. You will not find it in Mahayana, and definitely not in Shravakayana. In Vajrayana, there is that term and phenomenon of root guru. The notion of root guru is really related to a lot of things. In Vajrayana, one’s own emotion and one’s own element is used as the path. ^563

Depending on one’s own emotion and element, we also have the tendency to belong to different Buddha families like padma, ratna, vajra, karma, and Buddha. Even though you cannot separate these five Buddha families, one of them could be the prominent or the presiding element that you have. That then becomes the root family for your practice. Because of that, all this “root” business starts ⎯ the root guru, the root deity and all that. ^564

At times the root guru also refers to one who gives pointing-out instructions[65]. Many gurus point out, but if you actually have a glimpse of understanding the nature of mind through a specific guru’s pointing-out instructions, then that guru has so much connection with you. Therefore he or she can be very special for you. ^565

105. Can you talk about your root guru a little bit? ^566

For me even to claim that I have a root guru is a big arrogance because I don’t know whether I have ever been a student. To be a student, you really have to be a follower. I have never managed to be a follower. Therefore, I dare not stain the names of all my gurus by claiming that they are my root guru. By doing so, it could also be a disgrace to the tantric lineage. ^567

Relatively speaking, the gurus from whom I have received teachings are amazing. Even on a human level, they are the greatest humans that have walked on this earth. Having a student and product like me may not be the best thing to prove that my gurus were great, but I believe that even to mention the names of my gurus may be beneficial. ^568

I have received so many teachings from so many great masters. I have to single out Kyabje Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche[66] for his kindness, compassion, caring, humanness and trust. The trust that he has in me, without me even needing to win it, that alone is not repayable for billions of lifetimes. ^569

106. From a disciple’s point of view, how can we maintain samaya with a root guru? ^570

Try always to have the right attitude and pure perception. Perception is so important. One should also aspire and pray that one will be a good student. ^571

Keeping samaya in general and especially with a guru is difficult. Having a good foundation of hearing and contemplation, and also a little understanding of the view, such as the Madhyamika, always helps. ^572

Practically speaking, one can also be mindful in how one is with the guru. For instance, how much time one can spend with the guru and how close one should be with the guru. Also if the guru has other disciples, then the situation is much more difficult, because there are other human beings, and human beings have emotions. So while one has to get what one needs, one should also try not to make the situation too uncomfortable to others, and eventually to oneself. ^573

Like the precious text says, like a bee sucking honey from a flower, it does not really harm the flower, but gets what it needs and then flies away and spreads the pollen to create more flowers. That is how a student should try to keep samaya with the guru and his words. ^574

107. What kind of relationship should we expect to establish with our guru, or should we not expect anything at all? ^575

Learning not to expect is a very, very profound path, especially with a guru. But one’s expectation can be replaced by supplication and the aspiration that the guru will never drop us in the middle of the desert of samsara. Because we are all blind, the desert has no clear path and we are not equipped with a compass or map. Relying on the guru’s guidance, and aspiring and supplicating for it, is not necessarily expectation. Expectation is when we have done something for the guru, and expect that he does something for us in return. ^576

It is advised that one should always try to remember that the guru is the healer, we ourselves are the patients, and the dharma is the medicine. This kind of information is important, but it is difficult to remember because when we spend time with the guru, we forget, and we always end up of having the habit of being peers of equal standing. Similarly, one should consider the guru as the guide and the coach, but at the same time it is also important to have the notion that the guru is like family. In certain cases, this could also help. ^577

108. When we take refuge with our guru and the Three Jewels, it is said that we should rely on our guru. Many Buddhist students really rely on the guru to eliminate the obstacles in their life or career. If that turns out to be effective, they praise the guru and become more devoted; but if not, they may doubt that the guru is the Buddha if he can’t do that for them. Things like that make me want to ask Rinpoche, in what respects should students rely on the guru, and in what respects should we rely on our own practice? ^578

That kind of thinking is very theistic thinking. Taking refuge in the guru is actually taking refuge in the path. You have chosen to take refuge in this person to aid you in your path to selflessness, compassion and enlightenment, and to seeing the truth and dispelling delusion. So it’s a conscious decision to appoint someone to disrupt your life in the most profound sense. So from that point of view, when things really go wrong for your ego, you should be thanking the guru. ^579

109. As a Vajrayana practitioner, devotion to the guru is very important. Will devotion bring attachment? If it is not completely possible not to be at all attached to the guru, how should we balance it so that we are attached in a good way to our practice? ^580

I think that as deluded beings, we will always have some sort of human feelings towards the guru ⎯ as father, partner, saviour, companion and so on. Waiting for these feelings to go away in order to have a clear devotion, as it is described in the tantric texts, may not be the best idea. Instead, appreciate and acknowledge having these emotions, and appreciate them as a means to kindle or lead to more profound devotion and pure perception. That is what we should pray for. That way, seeming garbage and trash have become gold, silver and diamond. That is the alchemy of Buddhist practice ⎯ the skilful means of alchemy. ^581

110. In your recently published book Not for Happiness, you mentioned that merit plays a very important role in finding the right guru and that merit is also required at every stage of our relationship with a guru. Could you talk more about this, and teach us the best way to accumulate such merit? ^582

Especially for those who have no guru yet, Jamgon Kongtrul Lodro Thaye[67] advised us that, as a follower of the Buddha’s teachings, we already have a guru in common, and that is Sakyamuni himself. But of course, we wish to have a human guru to whom we can talk, and who can advise and correct us. Since we don’t have this human guru yet, for the time being we can pray to Sakyamuni Buddha and supplicate him to manifest a guru with whom we can communicate in person. That could also create lots of merit. ^583

On top of that, we can apply all the other methods of creating merit, such as practicing love, compassion, joy and equanimity; and making offerings of the seven limbs , which is paying homage to the Buddha and the bodhisattvas, making offerings, confessing our delusions, rejoicing in other people’s good deeds, asking the Buddhas to turn the wheel of dharma, asking them to remain insamsara for a long time, and dedicating the merit to all beings. ^584

111. Rinpoche, you do have many fanatic female disciples. Observing their emotional behaviour made me doubt their motivation and wonder if they really want to be enlightened or just to develop some kind of relationship with you. On the other hand, many of these women must have developed real devotion out of ordinary attraction to you. Is ordinary sentiment a particular obstacle for female disciples, and how do you manage to transform that? Do you have any special suggestion for female practitioners here? ^585

Someone told me I have so many crazy followers. Actually, I take that as a compliment, because if a hospital is full of patients, it means that the hospital is quite good. If I really try to practice the genuine bodhisattva path, I would say I still don’t have enough female followers and I wish there will be more. ^586

Generally for all the people, including all the female students, who are linked with me, we are brought together because of a karmic connection. I must have owed them a lot and they must have owed me a lot. Now that in this life I am following the holy path of the dharma, I hope and pray that all these links will eventually lead all of us to enlightenment. ^587

But it is not always like that because my mind does not always go to the bodhisattva path. When my mind is not percolated with bodhicitta, which is most of the time, then I feel that these followers, especially the female followers, must have a strong karmic debt with me in the past. It’s like how many of us have a strong karmic debt with the World Wrestling Federation, which is such a stupid program, but so many of us just feel compelled to watch it because we have a karmic debt. ^588

Ordinary sentiment and attraction can be an obstacle or an opportunity. As human beings, the most unexpected incident can trigger either negative or positive emotions, and all the events of our lives can lead to many other events , some harmful and others beneficial. An initial feeling of attraction is like that. ^589

So it is possible that a female student may initially be attracted to me for no spiritual reason, but because I am the opposite gender or because of some karmic connection. But a connection initiated like that can lead to more meaningful incidents. ^590

Conventionally we think of a serene Buddha drawing us to the spiritual journey. But sometimes it comes from the opposite, including being attracted to someone for their worldly rather than spiritual qualities. That could, however, lead somewhere profound and significant. ^591

Generally speaking, of course, sentiment is a product of the dualistic mind, and as such is an obstacle to the path. Having said that, however, a person always follows a path because he or she is attracted to something, and that attraction is a sentiment. ^592

I also want to point out that attraction comes from my side too. I don’t want to claim that I’m this cool person who never gets attracted ⎯ I do! I just hope and pray that my attraction, crush or infatuation will also lead me and others to something worthwhile. ^593

The picture of Sakyamuni Buddha is painted by all Buddhists as serene, gentle, majestic, golden and so on, and that is generally portrayed in the sutras too. Since we are attracted to the image of a serene, majestic, simple, humble monk as inspiration for the spiritual journey, therefore the Buddha manifested that way so that we will follow him. ^594

Now if the Buddha comes with a punk hairstyle dyed pink, with nose rings, lip rings, and tattoos all over, how many of us will follow this Buddha? Well, definitely not the traditional Buddhists. And indeed most of us might think that sinful. But it is a known fact there are probably more Lady Gaga followers on this earth than followers of the Buddha. So we cannot opt out by saying Buddha does not manifest this way. Buddha has to manifest for different needs. ^595

So sentiment is an obstacle, but that very sentiment may also be what brings you to the teaching. My conclusion is that if a female follower comes with a particular human sentiment, like the sentiment of wanting a relationship, then I will aspire not to be afraid of this sentiment, and not to reject her, even though it is really scary. ^596

Even one female coming with this sentiment of wanting a relationship is a big burden, so if there are hundreds, that’s a lot. But I pray that I will be brave enough to receive the sentiment and not reject it. I pray that I will make use of that sentiment and turn it into the right path. So please don’t hesitate to send me love letters and by the way, even from the boys. ^597

112. How should we regard the phenomenon of supernatural ability that could emerge in the practice of the Buddhadharma, and can we judge a guru by his supernatural capacities? Why are wisdom and compassion always emphasized in a guru but not supernatural ability? ^598

Supernatural ability, from the worldly point of view, is not at all a goal of Buddhist practice. If a guru’s prediction is accurate, if his puja works, if he can heal your outer disease, if he is more clairvoyant, if he can tell fortunes or read feng-shui accurately ⎯ none of that makes that guru even one point higher. It means nothing at all! Most probably that guru could deceive a lot of people. ^599

A guru must have the one and most important ability that matters, and that is to enlighten you. That’s the only capacity a guru should have. The rest are just details. Being able to fly, being able to give you a number to win the lottery, and being able to cook an omelette, are equally the same thing. ^600

113. Could Rinpoche talk about fake lamas and gurus? ^601

Not only in modern times, but even in the time of the Buddha, there were fake gurus. But fake gurus give us the challenge of going through the whole maze and trying to find the crack that enables us to find the right one. In fact, there are many fake gurus, and that is what makes life so interesting. ^602

Fake or not has a lot to do with how much knowledge the guru has, but that is actually a very minor way of judging. More important is how disciplined he is. When I say disciplined, I am not only talking about the outer disciplines, like whether he wears his robes properly or not, because that is less important. The more important disciplines are respecting the Three Jewels and the law of cause and condition and so on. ^603

Those disciplines are important because many gurus nowadays might teach or even sell things for the sake of money, power or fame. That actually shows that they don’t have respect for causes and conditions. The Buddha himself said that’s a very grave mistake and bad karma. ^604

Most importantly, the guru should be kind. The guru can be disciplined, but if the guru is not kind and learned, then I would consider him almost in the category of the fake gurus. ^605

Chapter 8: The Tulku System and Reincarnation ^606

114. Rinpoche, you are born as a Buddhist guru, so people are usually very interested how you were brought up? Could you tell us about your childhood? ^607

I am supposedly the incarnation of Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro, but I am not sure. Because looking at Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro and Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo, the predecessor of Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro, it is a disgrace to Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro and Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo not to be reincarnated, or reborn, as someone who is better or at least equal. Because if I am really the incarnation of Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo and Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro, certainly their enlightened qualities have worn out and all the negativity of emotions has come back. The law of karma can’t make that kind of mistake. ^608

Anyway, for what it’s worth, I am stuck with the name of the incarnation of Jamyang Khyentse. And I use the word “stuck” because I can’t cancel it. Even if I cancel it, it will still be there. That’s the unfortunate part. It doesn’t seem to have an expiry date. ^609

The word “tulku” means the manifested body or manifested form. The notion of manifested body or manifested form as a tulku is actually very much found in Mahayana Buddhist teachings. Bodhisattvas manifest as a form ⎯ as bird, butterfly, king, queen, politician, merchant, bridge, fan, pen ⎯ all sorts of things. Of all the tulkus, the most supreme is Sakyamuni Buddha, and so we call him supreme nirmanakaya[68] or supreme tulku. ^610

When we talk about manifestation, we are talking about the audience for whom the manifestation is supposed to manifest, for if there is no audience, the manifestation is useless. But when we talk about the current term tulku, which refers to Tibetan tulkus, even though this concept is rooted in that basic fundamental Mahayana philosophy, we have to be quite careful, because we are talking about a slightly different thing. ^611

The Tibetan tulku system is an institution. So it’s a bit like the words democracy and Democrat. Democracy is supposedly a very good thing, but not all Democrats — the people in one of the two parties that run the United States — are necessarily good. ^612

So the term tulku, as used for Tibetan lamas, is rooted in that very sublime notion of a manifestation body. This system had its purpose especially in places like Tibet, where once upon a time, religious institutions, especially Buddhist institutions, played quite an important role in the social context. Many monasteries were great seats of activity that served as the place politically, economically, and in art, medicine and more. Many, many people depended on those monasteries. ^613

So this is a very interesting phenomenon actually. For instance, Ganden Monastery in Tibet has one of the most sound tulku systems in the modern sense. Their Ganden Tripas are very high lamas, selected through their merit, education, what they have done, and what kind of training they have completed. So many of the Ganden Tripas are those who climbed the steps and reached the highest seat. ^614

Then, there are the Sakya and Mindrolling families, which run through the system of family lineage. And then we have the Karmapas, from which the tulku concept as known in Tibet emerged. ^615

Strictly from the Mahayana point of view, you cannot say that these recognized and enthroned tulkus are more authentic tulkus, and that unrecognized tulkus like birds, butterflies, dogs, hunters and prostitutes are not tulkus. ^616

In fact, more likely, it will be the other way round ⎯ that they are the real tulkus, and the people who are sitting on the thrones are not the real tulkus. We have to bear in mind that all those systems are corruptible. Even though the Ganden Tripa system does seem to be the least corruptible system, we know that with human beings, there is always corruption. ^617

Things become very murky and corruptible when a seat or lineage becomes very influential, because spiritual influence is always very big. Compared to Christianity, Judaism and Islam, Buddhist influence in society is nothing. But spiritual power can be huge! What the pope or a cardinal or bishop can do is really big. ^618

Similar things happen in Tibet, on a much smaller scale but still with considerable influence. For instance, many monastic seats, especially ancient ones, also have political and economic influence, because there is also a connection between these worldly kinds of influence and spiritual influence. In fact, actions in those worldly spheres were often initiated with a good intention and purpose. ^619

The Mongols and Manchus were in search of spiritual blessings and teachings, and they became patrons of the great lamas. Then, somehow, they offered seals, power, and more, and then certain lamas became the royal priests of the Yuan and Qing dynasties. All these things became important phenomena for ordinary people. I don’t think the Buddha would be impressed if the Manchus gave him a seal or stamp, because Sakyamuni Buddha, our big boss, left the palace, and didn’t have a hat or a seal. ^620

However, there are some amazing accounts of tulkus. For instance, whenPemaLedrelTsal[69] — Pema Sal’s[70] incarnation — was dying, he told his disciples that “I am going to come back in five years”. Hehadn’t finished some of his teachings, and he said “you must give me back my teachings when I come back”. Sure enough, he came back as Longchenpa[71]. Stuff like that is wonderful, amazing. ^621

I was told that in Sichuan in China, a big Buddha was built by one monk in three lifetimes. Like that, there are lots of remarkable contributions that tulkus have made ⎯ in fact tulkus who are not necessarily high ranking. ^622

Over the years, however, the tulku system has become more and more corrupted, and now it is so corrupted that it is almost useless. If one has any sense, one will not even claim that one is a tulku. ^623

115. Rinpoche, you mentioned that a guru should not only be learned in Buddhism but also really understand the suffering of sentient beings. You are so welcomed because you are one of us, and not so far away. In your life, what do you think is the main reason why you get to know your disciples so well, and to understand their happiness and sufferings? ^624

I don't believe that I understand the suffering of people. I hope I do, I really want to, and I think that any responsible teacher should learn that. That’s very important ⎯ in fact probably the most important thing for a teacher to know. The doctor must learn the symptom of the patient. Otherwise the doctor is useless. ^625

In fact I hope I will know the sufferings not just of human beings, but also of all sentient beings; and not only knowing them, but I hope I can have sympathy and empathy. I honestly am saying that it is not easy. It hasn’t happened to me all this time. ^626

Once I was in Morocco, and saw a small donkey carrying this cart with lots of luggage. And only for a moment I thought this donkey could have been my sister in my past life. And then I gave that donkey some fruit, which the donkey ate. After leaving the donkey, I had this feeling that this may have been the last time in this life to see this donkey, because it was in a bazaar in the middle of Morocco. And that evening I was thinking what the donkey must be doing now, was it being treated well by his owner and so on. ^627

And then the next morning came, breakfast arrived, and it was forgotten.  Sometimes when I look at the photo of the donkey I took, I feel sort of sad. I hope that this kind of feeling happens to me often, but it doesn’t ⎯ my mind has become as numb as a serial killer’s mind. ^628

116. The tulku system sounds mysterious. What has to be achieved in one’s practice in order to come to our world through the power of aspiration instead of being drawn here by karmic wind? ^629

Ideally, all the tulkus’ manifestations come as a result of aspiration and compassion. But for people like me, I wish there was a choice. I am like a feather in the wind, with no choice but to be pushed and pulled by circumstances and situations. ^630

Of all the circumstances and situations that blow you, the worst is religious institutions. This is why it makes sense that the great Tsele Natsok Rangdrol[72] in one of his prayers prayed never to reborn as a high lama or tulku of an important and big monastery, because being a high lama of a monastery deceives oneself and others through spiritual materialism. Sometimes I think I would rather be reborn as a prostitute somewhere in the city, where I could benefit beings without all this spiritual materialistic corruption. ^631

117. Could Rinpoche talk about the role that the tulku reincarnation system plays in the propagation and spread of Vajrayana Buddhism? ^632

l’d like to believe that the tulku system does contribute to the propagation of Vajrayana;but strictly speaking, I don't know how much tulkus are helping or harming. This is what Jamyang Khyentse Chokyi Lodro said: “It is the tulkus who will propagate the dharma and who will stabilize the dharma. It is also the tulkus who will destroy the dharma.” And he is so right, because especially nowadays there are just so many fake tulkus ⎯ so many! ^633

The only good thing about the fake tulkus is that they make life interesting. But it‘s also so scary, because the unfortunate thing is that all these fake tulkus not only know how to approach the masses, but being fake, they put extra effort into selling themselves. And we human beings are so stupid that we always fall for that. Logically speaking, if somebody is trying hard to sell himself, to me it already shows a little bit of dodginess here. But it doesn’t work like that. We actually fall for it. It’s so sad. ^634

118. Could you tell us how young tulkus are brought up nowadays? ^635

Our previous generation, like Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche’s generation, went through so much. They basically had to move away from their dwelling and land ⎯ that must be just so shocking ⎯ and yet they never gave up the dharma, they taught dharma month after month, year after year, inside a tent. They groomed and trained people inside the tent, eating just potatoes and drinking just hot water, year after year. ^636

And then comes our generation, who got so much scolding from these lamas for being so spoiled, so lazy and so on, which in those days we couldn’t understand. After all, we grew up without all these glittering gold monasteries we have today, with their beautiful dining rooms, carpets, cars, telephones, bedrooms, flush toilets and more ⎯ none of that existed. I remember walking from the border of India to Lumbini, which is in Nepal, for two days, just to catch a bus so that I could go to Kathmandu to receive teachings from Karmapa ⎯ things like that. ^637

There is that generation, but if you tell this to the younger generations today, they think it’s just a myth, or they don't even believe you, because they just have not seen that earlier lifestyle. I think most of the young generation tulkus nowadays have never even put their butt in a bus, let alone having to walk. The irony is that I think the young tulkus today are being abused in a very, very strange way. ^638

I am not even talking about getting beaten up, which actually happened to us. I got beaten so many times, and on the head too till it was bleeding⎯that was the tradition of training. I also deserved that, because I was very wild. But now, not only don’t the tulkus get that, they are so sheltered. Even in taking 100 steps, the tulkus go with 14 different attendants. If it is 200 steps, it is in a car ⎯ usually a very good car. ^639

So for the first 20 or 25 years, they end up being protected so much from the rest of the world. And then the tulkus’ hormones grow, which you cannot control because it’s a biological thing, and they begin to see things to which they don't know how to relate. So what’s happening with the tulku system right now, ironically, is that the monasteries, labrangs, and all that are making these tulkus know nothing about real life, and so the tulkus grow up not knowing how to relate to the world of 2014. ^640

Because of how the human psyche works, I am afraid many of these young tulkus will grow up so isolated, and get frustrated and suffer. In our days, we got really punished, but we got trained, so that when we reached our 20s and 30s, we were at least equipped with a bit of knowledge and knew what’s happening out there. Now, most of them don't know. They have never been rejected. Sharing is never in their heads. Everything is served. ^641

Now I am beginning to hear from a few young tulkus whose hormones are growing, so they like girls. But they don't know how to date, and they don't even know how to be polite. These young lamas are used to ordering people around, and demanding and getting what they want. So they just don’t know how to have a relationship, because in a relationship you can’t always order your partner around. That never works, because a relationship is like a tango dance and it takes two to tango! ^642

119. In order to better promote Buddhism in modern society, do you think the current tulku system needs to be adjusted? If so, what should be preserved and what should be changed? ^643

I am sure the tulku system needs a lot of adjustment. But before any adjustment, we need to talk about these things, and nobody is presently talking about them. I would like to meet with the young tulkus’ monasteries, labrangs and tutors, and talk about these things. But they pretend it is not happening, like the ostrich that, when something dangerous is happening, digs a hole in the earth and hides its head in it. At least the ostrich knows the danger is coming, and that’s the only thing it knows to do. In the tulkus’ case, they don't even know the problem. ^644

You may think I am exaggerating, but I am actually underestimating. There are tulkus who don't know how to pick up food from a buffet because they have always been served. This is complicated by all that guru devotion stuff from the students. ^645

So what’s happening is that monasteries and labrangs are basically destroying these young human beings and depriving these kids of their human abilities. How are you going to expect to liberate all sentient beings when you cannot put on your own socks? ^646

And recently there are some young tulkus suddenly acting like Chogyam Trungpa Rinpoche, as if they are revolting against the system. But then their revolution and their revolt is a little strange. It is a bit like me trying to paint Picasso’s paintings. Picasso did all these crazy things, but we know that Picasso could do good, traditional paintings and yet went beyond that, which is what makes Picasso special. ^647

Trungpa Rinpoche was very well trained and accomplished, and he knew how to behave according to social norms; and yet he went beyond that. He was special. But most of the young tulkus who are emulating Trungpa Rinpoche, or trying to be revolutionary, are revolting towards something so superficial, are not developed themselves, and will soon make fools of themselves. ^648

Chapter 9: Buddhism and Education, Society, and Arts ^649

120. When you look at the modern education system for young kids, do you think that we are providing what they really need? What can Buddhism offer to help children, especially in China? ^650

Education is an unfortunate phenomenon in many ways, because education is a form of brainwashing. And whenever we brainwash, we have an agenda. In our agendas, we always think that whatever we value is the best ⎯ a particular system, religion, political ideology, economic structure, whatever. Somebody, somewhere, a long time ago decided that gold is good, so that’s why everybody values and searches for gold. ^651

But even though education is a very unfortunate phenomenon, it is inevitable and is going to go on whether it is fortunate or not. With that in mind, the best thing we can do is really to have a big picture, long vision and kind heart. ^652

Cherishing and being concerned not only about ourselves is really the root of kindness. The bigger picture is not only thinking about our grandchildren, but also that the fish have to live in the ocean, that the butterflies have to fly, and that the whole world has to function. So with that kind of big vision in mind, ideally I think that education should be reformed, but I am afraid it is not going to happen. ^653

It’s very difficult to reform the education system with that kind of vision in mind, because governments are too afraid to change, and because they would make a lot of people jobless by doing so. A lot of consumers and factory owners will get angry, and car companies will hate us for coming up with this type of education system. Our education system is basically influenced by the materialistic world. ^654

And then the parents will also resist such change. I myself have experienced this when I asked parents to send their kids to a classical dance school in South India, where they mainly teach the children how to dance. The immediate thought in their head is how are my children going to survive, and that is a very understandable and respectable response. ^655

So when I tell parents to do that, I do it with a lot of courage. Little do people realize that when their kids go to top universities, it doesn’t necessarily guarantee that they will get a job. So many of my friends’ kids completed their studies in Ivy League universities in the United States, and still have not found the right job. But blind hope is what drives people. So I think that when it comes to a kind heart and big vision, Buddhism can really influence and help. ^656

121. What in Buddhism should be made available to children? ^657

Buddhism can contribute lots and lots of things. For instance, do kids know we have something called mind? That’s a very big question, and we keep missing it. Do the American schools teach that? Do the Chinese schools teach that? Kids study biology and anatomy, and are taught that most people have two feet, one nose and two hands, and that each hand has five fingers. But there is something called mind that’s actually the most important and most powerful. ^658

We can teach kids that others have feelings just like we do, and others get jealous just like we do. And we can teach kids there are always consequences in doing whatever they do. The toilet paper that we wipe out bottoms with has the consequence of chopping down trees, and the car you drive may cause lung cancer in your grandchildren. ^659

Then we can teach them about dependent arising. We can teach them about love and compassion. Then we can teach them that morality is so important, and eventually we can teach them that we should not be stuck with morality. ^660

122. Do you think it is a good idea to establish Buddhist kindergartens, elementary schools, and even universities, to combine Buddhist teachings with the sciences, humanities and technology? ^661

Yes, but I think that we have to be careful here, because we don’t want to make Buddhism into some sort of organized cult. I think Buddhist values and wisdom are important, but if there is a Buddhist kindergarten, school or university, the purpose should not be to make more Buddhists. Their purpose should be to make more kind, visionary, caring, refined, creative, elegant and outrageous people. ^662

123. If you had all the resources needed to establish a kindergarten, how would you like it to be? ^663

This is of course a dream. If I ever have the opportunity to create a school for children, I want them to learn what it is to be human. Because of the way things are manifesting now in the world, our children are forgetting that we are human beings, not only metaphorically but literally. Our children are beginning to act like robots and to think that others are like robots, so they have no qualms and don’t care about pulling a trigger to shoot someone. ^664

I want to teach children how to make a fire, and that the source of water does not come from the tap. The aim of this education is to refine ourselves so that we will see the world in a different way, so that we can help others, and through helping others make ourselves happy and content. Therefore, what we are learning is not to get jobs, but to refine and make ourselves elegant both outwardly and inwardly. ^665

Our children can learn not to be boring and to take pride in thinking that not everybody has to wear a pair of Prada shoes. To do that, I would like to create an atmosphere at the school, so that classes can be taught under a big tree, by the bank of a river or in the paddy field; and so that lessons could involve getting up at three in the morning and watching the colour of the sky, and listening to the sound of silence. The uniform of this school can be a variety of different costumes in the world. But this is me being romantic. It is never going to be realized. ^666

124. You know the “Tiger-parents” phenomenon in China. Parents try very hard to cultivate their kids in the hope they will be successful in the future. The competition is getting more and more intense nowadays — even before elementary school, young kids start to learn all sorts of things. Could you give these parents some advice? ^667

To a certain extent, I actually really like the idea. However, I think what’s important here is that the Tiger-parents must be prepared to be tigers all the way. There is no point being a tiger for 20 years and then leaving the kids in the desert, where there will be different kinds of species to which the tigers and their cubs are not accustomed. ^668

What if the Tiger-parents really coach their kids so well for 20 years, and then after 20 years, the world has changed completely, so that everything that you have coached and aimed for really doesn’t do any good? That will be so sad, because in a way, when you are young and a child is the right time to have fun. With the Tiger-parents, you lose that fun. By the time you reach age 20, you have already missed that childhood fun, and now you are going to miss the rest of the fun too, so you are actually going to lose all the fun. ^669

For instance, I have seen a lot of kids of my generation, kids who have so much to talk about when they grew up in the woods, swimming in the river, and not in school, and often what they are saying is very humane. And then, when I travel to a place like Hong Kong or Singapore, most of the kids have nothing to share except the newest application and software ⎯ nothing personal. ^670

125. How can we best deal with or educate teenagers? ^671

I have no specific answer to this, but maybe it is good to keep in our minds that, even within the short time of just ten years from now, the world will change quite dramatically. The way we live and communicate will change, the environment is changing, and there will be advanced technologies that can make our lives faster, more efficient and more economical. That also means challenges will become faster, more efficient and more complicated. ^672

So our children in about ten years’ time will have a different kind of suffering, both mentally and physically. And this is something of which we should be aware ⎯ as parents, as a society and as governments. Even to have that awareness without really having a practical answer, I think, can help. ^673

There is a general assumption especially in Asian societies that our children love their parents. I don’t know how much of this value will continue. Already in the west, you see many modern kids who not only do not love their parents, but in fact have aversion to them, and these teenagers’ aversion towards their parents often has a very good reason. ^674

I have encountered so many of my friends, especially in Asia, who, as parents, give everything to their children and then after so many years, the children grow up rebelling, which then pushes the parents into the most depressing situation. So it is also important to have some wisdom in the way we raise our own beloved children. ^675

126. From your point of view, what is a perfect parental relationship like? Could you talk about how young people in China could learn to become good parents? Will Buddhism help to improve family relationships? ^676

Probably I am being very biased. But as a Buddhist myself, I would say that when children grow up in a Buddhist environment, in whichthe parents are practicing Buddhists, have pictures of the Buddha, listen to the teachings, and utter words like love, compassion, forgiveness and wisdom, surely there must be some effect. At least that’s much better than in places like Afghanistan, where all they might talk about is guns, and where kids who are shorter than an AK47 carry an AK47. ^677

Of course, it’s very individual. I would not say that just because we have Buddhist families, the kids will turn out very well. In fact, it is very important not to force your kids to be Buddhists. That openness and not forcing our views on others is one of the prides and skills of Buddhism, and that should be maintained. ^678

127. Nevertheless, do you think that people who don’t believe in Buddhism are hopeless? ^679

No, they are not really hopeless as human beings, especially when there are many other religions that are equally profound and good, like Jainism and some of the Hindu schools, for example. In China’s case, for example, people who are influenced by Taoist philosophy and wisdom are a great hope for the country, because the Taoists dare to talk about the world that is beyond this world. That is very valuable. ^680

128. Why do you think that China is important for Buddhism? Do you have any suggestions for modern China to embrace Buddhism to provide solutions for our many social problems? ^681

I may be wrong, but I would say not only now, but even historically, China may be the only country or one of the few countries where Buddhism went to the grassroots level. Even among the very few where this occurred, we certainly know that China is the greatest nation that embraced Buddhism at the societal level. Even in Buddhism’s birth country, India, Buddhism never really went down to the grassroots level. ^682

As well, China has traditionally been the most generous and powerful patron of Buddhism, probably even more than Harsha[73], Ashoka, and Kanishka[74] put together. This is a subject to ponder and research on. ^683

Of course, there are countries like Burma, Bhutan and Sri Lanka that claim to be Buddhist countries, but the scale is very small. When I talk about Buddhism going into the backbone, the genes and the DNA at the grassroots level, I am talking about things like the most anti-religious Chinese communist party member drinking Iron Guan Yin tea even at the height of the Cultural Revolution. I have yet to see a dish in India that is named after a bodhisattva. ^684

It may not seem that important, but having these things can always trigger a question. One day people will ask “who is Guan Yin?” With the philosophy behind this simple question, you could find yourself in the world of the great compassion of Avalokitesvara. ^685

There is also something new in China that I think is really good. In the past, Chinese Buddhists were maybe more devotion oriented, but now the Chinese are more critical. Even with all this critical thinking, the Chinese are actually feeling at home with Buddhism, and Buddhism is prevailing in China. ^686

As much as China can benefit a lot from the wisdom of Buddhism, Buddhism can also benefit a lot from China. Buddhism has been abused and still is being abused by the west, by Christians, and by Muslims. Buddhists don’t have any voice. Any western journalist will draw a cartoon making fun of the Buddha without even blinking his eyes. There are big Buddha statues in the Buddha bar. In Paris, I saw a Buddha statue with a begging bowl that is used to hold restaurant menus. Have you heard any outcry from Buddhists? ^687

I am not suggesting that we should have an outcry, but I am saying that it would be interesting to have about five senators in the US who are practicing Buddhism and who are openly Buddhists. If there were five US senators who were Buddhists, I think the paradigm would be shifted. If half of the Zhongnanhai members were Buddhists, probably there will be a good chance that the environment will be saved. At least on the intellectual level, people will then talk about love and compassion, and that’s important. This is me talking not as a spiritual person but as a blind, patriotic Buddhist who wants to see Buddhism grow, but on a more worldly level. ^688

129. Recently some western researchers predicted that in thirty years China will have the world’s largest Christian community. What does Rinpoche think about that? ^689

As Buddhists, we believe in karma. So where causes and conditions gather, results appear. So this prediction is very possible if certain causes and conditions come together in China. After all, some predominantly Islamic countries like Afghanistan, Indonesia and Pakistan used to be Buddhist countries, and now there is hardly a trace of Buddhism there. ^690

At the same time, who would have thought that a 2,500-year-old seemingly archaic Asian religion like Buddhism would flourish in Europe and America, but it is. In fact, it is becoming most popular there among intellectuals and thinkers who use reason and not just faith. ^691

To me this is very encouraging, first because Buddhism has no tradition at all of forcing people to become Buddhists. In fact, Buddhists have never waged war in the name of religion or to convert others to Buddhism. But neither does Buddhism lure or bribe adherents by giving them blankets and building hospitals. Indeed, Buddhism is so poor that it couldn’t even afford to do this. ^692

So the present influx of western intellectuals and lovers of reason to Buddhism is a source of pride for Buddhism, because it means that thinkers and educated people are determined to find what is right and true. If Christianity or any other religion flourishes in China because the Chinese people have used their reason and intellect to decide that this is the answer for their lives, then that is wonderful and indeed admirable. ^693

But I myself have found the Chinese people I know to be so individual and pragmatic that I personally think they will feel more at home with the Buddhist teachings that you are your own master. ^694

130. Some people try to combine religions like Christianity and Buddhism.  What does Rinpoche think about that? ^695

That is useless, stupid, and deprives both traditions. First of all, you simply cannot join Buddhism with Christianity, Islam or other religions. I am not saying Buddhism is better, but just that it is different. In fact, such differences are the very wealth of humanity, giving us different views, philosophies and religions from which to choose. The left direction and right direction cannot be combined and mixed into one direction, or you will not get anywhere at all. You should just choose one direction and respect those who go the other way. ^696

131. Do you have any suggestions for young Buddhists in China on how they canhelp Buddhism grow? Besides philanthropic effort, what can we do? ^697

Buddhists’ strength is the appreciation of reasoning and critical thinking. It has always been that. If you learn about the great ancient universities like Nalanda, it is clear that the Buddha himself propagated critical thinking. In China or anywhere else, for that matter, people should not forget that. For the survival of Buddhism, this commitment to reasoning and critical thinking is probably more important than philanthropic activity. I would rather have a monastery with a proper academic curriculum and study of critical thinking than a monastery that gives free soup. ^698

As I said earlier, young people should realize that to be Buddhist does not necessarily mean they have to become monks or give up what they have and go join a monastery. They can still listen to rock and roll, go to dances, have fun and all of that, of course. And even though they may not be able to help others, they can at least try not to harm others. They can keep their careers and yet follow the Buddhadharma. This is something that is quite important to realize. ^699

132. Now I’d like to ask your attitude towards some controversial social issues. First, what do you think about gays and lesbians? ^700

Personally I have no thoughts about this. If you are asking what is the view of Buddhism about gays and lesbians, then I just need to repeat what I’ve said many times before,  that the main goal of the Buddhadharma is to really understand the truth and to be free from delusion. Delusion means looking at an illusion and thinking it is the truth. So to be free from that kind of delusion is the main thing. I don’t think that our sexual orientation will make much difference in that quest. I cannot say that a straight person has a better chance to see the truth than gays and lesbians. In fact, gays and lesbians are often much more sensitive, which could also help their journey. ^701

But we have to keep in mind that, like all other religions, Buddhism is also delivered through the medium of culture. So a culture that has prejudices towards gays and lesbians may interpret homosexuality as immoral. But that is an issue in human culture, and has got nothing to do with Buddhism. ^702

133. What do you think about prostitution? ^703

There are so many prejudices in our human world. We have this strange prejudice against things like prostitution. On the other hand, we look up to professions such as lawyers or politicians. But many times, we don’t know who is really doing the immoral thing. ^704

I am totally against forced prostitution. In fact, not just prostitution, but any kind of forced activity is a cause of pain, and is inhuman. I cannot really give you any black and white answer here, but I’d like to think of it this way. An ambitious doctor can give a prescription, and may even be selling the medicine; and to make more money, he then tells the patient he has to get more medicine than he needs. Even though in reality that may not be true, the patient has no choice but to take what has been prescribed. And then we have this desperate woman in the street selling her own body, even if nobody is forcing her, and she is luring and taking advantage of people’s emotions. Sometimes we have to sit down and think which of these is worse. Probably they are equal. ^705

134. What do you think about drugs? ^706

There are many different types of drugs. In Buddhism, anything that intoxicates you or alters your mind using an external substance is not advised. It goes without saying that many of these drugs can be very harmful to your body, to your mind, and eventually to your family and society. ^707

But we also have to think about other things. In every part of the world, alcohol is socially accepted, and often institutionalized and even celebrated historically. Sometimes I think it would be interesting to see research on how many are damaged by alcoholism and smoking, physically or spiritually, compared to drugs, because I doubt it would be less than the damage caused by drug abuse. If other drugs are banned, perhaps lifting a cigarette or a champagne class should equally be banned along with the advertisements that promote them. ^708

135. What do you think about abortion and contraception? ^709

I can never tell people not to have sex. If you are a monk or a nun, you have taken that vow, so obviously I don’t have to tell them they should not have sex. But for others, we should not encourage having indefinite multiple partners, not only from a spiritual, but also from a health point of view. ^710

Now I have been asked many times what to do by people who got pregnant and want to abort this being. If you are asking what Buddhists would say about this, then it’s straightforward ⎯ you are basically killing a being. And in fact, you are killing your own child, so the karmic consequence can be very grave. ^711

For the sake of practicality, I’d like to advise people, especially the younger generations, that they should be really careful before they go out having fun, so that you don’t make someone pregnant or get pregnant. The fact that you have aborted a being that grew within your body can haunt you for a long time. What would you think if you yourself were meant to be aborted? ^712

136. Could Rinpoche talk about the arts in general? How can artists like painters, musicians, writers, singers and dancers be creative in a good and inspirational way? What can Buddhism provide to help them in this aspect? ^713

Not just for arts, but for politics, economics and everything else, the moment we begin these endeavours with the right motivation, I consider it a success already. Success is not measured by a job finished; success is measured by the job beginning with the right motivation. If a job is started with the right motivation, even if the job is not even 1% finished, Mahayana will consider this a success. Anyway, what does “job finished” mean? The job will never finish, and it should not finish. So I think artists, lamas, monks, politicians, policemen, nurses and soldiers  must all try to engage their endeavours with the right motivation. ^714

What is the right motivation? Fundamentally, the right motivation is measured by how much less selfishness there is and how much you think more about others. When I say others, it is not only your own nephews, cousins, sons and family, but also your village. Even more than your village, it is your country. Even more than your country, it is the whole world. That is how to approach the arts and everything else. ^715

Besides that basic motivation, art has a very powerful role because we often don’t know how to express and interpret things with the very limited tools and language that we have. For instance, we try to express our love with very limited methods. Yet love is so profound and so infinite. When love happens, something extraordinary happens ⎯ something that you cannot measure, value or quantify. It may be very short-lived, but when it happens, it’s like an experience of the satisfaction you get during yawning. There is a certain feeling that is so present and so vivid, yet it’s nothing that you can express. ^716

This is one example where art, music, poetry and dance can better portray an experience than our usual limited language and tools. In our lives, there are so many such valuable things that are worth expressing, and that can be beneficial not just to a few people, but to a lot. Art and music, I believe, can help to express them. ^717

Q: How can we be more creative with the right motivation? ^718

That’s very difficult, because to be creative means to step out of the zone we usually inhabit in. That’s so uncomfortable. It gets even more difficult, because if you do completely step out of the zone, and if your art intends to communicate with others, there may be no others with whom to communicate. So it’s like one foot should be inside the zone and the other foot should be outside the zone. There are not many people who want to be in that kind of situation. ^719

To be creative, one also needs to be very honest. Honesty is very difficult because we are accustomed to not think honestly. We have so many inhibitions, and all that is going to bog down our creativity. To communicate even partially, you have to bow down to other people’s way of communication and yet, at the same time, you want to make them know something they don’t already know. That is always difficult. ^720

137. Why are you interested in movies, and why are movies important to you? ^721

I don’t know whether movies are even important anymore, because there are so many other media now that movies are probably already very out-dated. But I think there must be lots of reasons for which I like movies. Most of those reasons are so selfish, worldly and materialistic, completely similar to those of many other people in the world. ^722

We like images, and especially images that move and make sounds. Then, of course, being able to tell a story and distract people for even five minutes, you feel a sense of triumph and victory that you managed to deceive people and lead them to some land of fantasy. That must be it. ^723

Certainly I don’t have an ambition to make money from film, because if I did, I wouldn’t be making the type of film I am making. It is not because I have no interest in making money through film or because I am a renunciant with no attachment to money. I just feel that it is so difficult to make a movie that makes money. ^724

People say things like “oh, what a trashy film”, “why do people go to such a trashy film?”, “what a bad film”, “it’s a blockbuster, and people spend so much money to see it”. But those films are very difficult to make, because you have to really know what people want, which is really difficult. You have to make a stereotypical movie, and at the same time it has to be interesting, because the audience is tough, and they are not idiots. ^725

People talk about how sex, violence and car chase movies sell, but that’s not necessarily so. You can have 90 minutes of sex in a film and it could be a complete flop. You really have to know how to deliver the story. I do still laugh at Hindi and Indian films today, but if somebody asks me to make one, it would be difficult for me. I don’t know the grammar or the language of that kind of film. ^726

I have always thought that maybe one day, if I encounter an idiot who wants to throw away about USD 100 million, I may be able to make a film on the life of the Buddha. That is just one of my very patriotic, humane, ambitious moods coming in the disguise of a devoted Buddhist. Who knows, by making such a film, I may not even benefit one single sentient being. ^727

But from time to time, there is the urge of wanting to do that⎯ the urge of really wanting to present the life of the Buddha not only to Buddhists, but to the larger audience ⎯ not to give them a correct picture, because you cannot do that, but at least some sort of picture of the Buddha. So there is that wish. Other than that, I definitely don’t have an interest in having a film career, because it is so tough. ^728

I personally do like films. I am always stuck with one or two films that I like the most and that never seems to go away. Some of the films are quite powerful, such as Andrei Tarkovsky’s Stalker, which is one of the most powerful films ever made to this day. ^729

If you ask me why I like Stalker, I actually don’t know how to express why I like it, but vaguely I think it is one of the bravest films ever made, and it is also very spiritual. I am sure I am interpreting it in my own way. The concept and the premise of the film are very interesting to me. Of course, this is not at all your usual film with all the stereotyped heroes, suspense and romance, etc. But I feel this film has a lot of symbolic meaning. It has a lot to do with how difficult it can get fitting into a society and trying to be within your normal zone if you think exceptionally and beyond a certain zone. But this could be entirely my own interpretation. ^730

Chapter 10: About death ^731

138. Earlier you spoke of impermanence. Everyone has to face death, including the death of one’s relatives and friends and the death of oneself. To worldly people, death is not auspicious, so people even try to avoid talking about it. But in Vajrayana, death is very thoroughly discussed and elaborately addressed. There are methods provided for us to utilize death as an opportunity to improve our practice. Rinpoche, please tell us how death is treated in Vajrayana? What attitude should we establish towards death? ^732

It’s always so important to talk about things that are certain to happen. The moment when people begin to hide things that are certain, something is going terribly wrong. You probably don't want to discuss death during a new year’s day celebration or on somebody’s birthday. That’s understandable, because death is regarded as unfortunate. And the reason why it is seen as unfortunate is partly because we don't want to lose what we have. But also a big part is that we don't know what death is. Actually many times we are trying to avoid talking about something very certain, but we are also trying to avoid something of which we are not aware. ^733

With my own eyes, I have seen a big difference between people who have thought about death and how they will handle death when they die, and people who totally pretend there is no death ⎯ I mean people who really don't want to face it. I had a friend in Hong Kong, an old lady, who has since passed away. Once I went to Hong Kong after my mother’s funeral. Usually she would come to see me or I would go to see her when I was in Hong Kong, but this time she didn’t want to either, because I had just come back from a funeral. For her, that was inauspicious. ^734

Even though we may not have in-depth knowledge of dying and death, it will always bring us a lot of awareness just to discuss its certainty, and also the uncertainty of when it is going to come. I bet our attitude will be different even in the way we shop, the way we build our houses, and the way we plan our life. ^735

And that will have an effect on the economy, because there is really no point saving to live forever when we know that’s just not going to happen. Then, if you have extra money, maybe you’ll know where to spend it. I am not even talking about building stupas or doing retreat, but about going to Africa, going to Australia, and watching the sunset. There are a lot of things we can do but we don't do because we are all preparing to live a long time. All of us think the real living is coming in a few years’ time. ^736

Now if you think about it, from kindergarten to university, education is all about arrangements to live. Earlier you were asking about kindergarten and children’s education. Well, I think that “death” should be one of the biggest subjects right from the beginning. ^737

Some people have this attitude of what’s the point of knowing what happens after death. In a way, it is true that we don't know what happens after death, because nobody who has died has come back to tell us. In a lot of what we are preparing, we are preparing for something that we imagine might happen. What if it doesn’t happen? But likewise, what if everything taught in the Buddhadharma, especially the bardo[75] teachings in Vajrayana, is actually true? What if, when your body and mind are separated, the mind goes to a completely different kind of experience? ^738

It's a bit like you are so used to walking around in shoes, and then suddenly you have no shoes, and you have to go bare-footed next door, to the grocery store,  you would have a different feeling. Suddenly your mind is naked, and you don't use things like eyes, ears, nose and body, let alone shoes and cars, so then your mind has a different sensation and a different perception that are probably new. ^739

It’s something so new, and yet you cannot visit there just for a short time and then go back to the safety zone, because there is no more zone ⎯ you have left the body. And this new experience can be very scary unless you already have some information. It’s like going to Kathmandu at night — if you don't know what to expect, it’s most scary. ^740

139. What will we experience during death and after death? Are the experiences of good practitioners any different than ordinary people? And what does death really mean for practitioners and non-practitioners? ^741

There is a big difference. If you are a good practitioner, you have a lot of information. The information may be wrong, but at least you have some sort of information. When you are not a practitioner, you have no information, so you are forced, for the first time in your life, to go to a territory you have never been before. Are you prepared for that? ^742

What happens during death all depends on different circumstances:  the cause of death, the way death happens, and how prepared you are ⎯ in other words, how much information you have. Death is an inevitably big part of life. In fact, in Buddhism, death is happening all the time. Living is dying. ^743

When you have the right information and when you are prepared, at least you are prepared to let go of things, to let go of your plans, ambitions, goals, concerns and disputes. If you don’t believe in a next life, then you are basically like the evaporation of a drop of water, so there is probably not much to think about. But what if you continue ⎯ not your body, but your consciousness, continues ⎯ then it makes sense to prepare ourselves. ^744

We can habituate this mind with kindness, compassion, creativity, sobriety and sanity. And then of course, if you are a follower of a spiritual path, you would like to influence your mind so that your mind will be spiritual. For a practitioner, death is like a reminder, and it is also an opportunity. For a non-practitioner, death is taboo and is something we deny. So it is good to be prepared for death so that we can live fully. ^745

140. Could Rinpoche please address what methods are provided in Vajrayana for us to prepare for death. ^746

In a way, not only Vajrayana, but all the yanas and all the teachings of the Buddha are never designed to make our present life comfortable or successful. They are all really aiming for beyond this life. Of course, to attain enlightenment within this lifetime if possible; but if not, at least in future lives. Specifically in Vajrayana, there is a teaching called bardo, which is a systematic teaching on how to actualize the truth, and how not to get caught by illusions and delusions. ^747

141. Practicing these methods, when can we say that we are ready for death? When will we know for sure that we will have control when we die? ^748

When we have no more regrets or when we feel we are prepared to die. Being prepared for the next life actually is important. ^749

When a person is not too engrossed in things like fame, gain, criticism or loss; when a person is not too bothered by being ignored or not too excited by having too much attention; and when a person is effortlessly thinking of Buddha, Dharma and Sangha, and guru, and aspects of the dharma effortlessly, without needing to put any effort into it, then it indicates this person will have a little bit control when he or she dies. ^750

142. Nowadays in modern hospitals, it is very hard to die quietly. Doctors often try their best to save dying people, and their efforts could be very distracting, especially to a practitioner. Does that mean that dying quietly at home is the best way for a practitioner? ^751

It really depends, but for a practitioner, dying quietly is very good. As Milarepa said, “when I get sick, if there is nobody who is asking how I am, and when I die, if there is nobody crying over my death and if I manage to die in this isolated place, I, Milarepa’s life-long wish is fulfilled.” This is his song. So, yes, the general rule is that it is good for a practitioner to die quietly. ^752

If the dying person is a seasoned practitioner, it would be good if the relatives leave this person alone as much as possible. If he is not a seasoned practitioner, then at least the relatives and friends definitely should not bring up issues that can make the dying person worry, like the money someone owes him, or his children’s exam failures, or how the children will divide the inheritance, or the arrival of his favourite ice-cream in the shop next door. Instead, the relatives should remind him to be calm, and to think of the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha. ^753

Here I also want to mention one thing that may be besides the point,  and that is that parents really should write a good will. I have seen so many cases of children going through so many difficulties because their parents, clinging to eternal life, forgot to prepare a good will, and then after the parents have left, the children fight bitterly. And I may be wrong here, but even though there is so much apparent equality in China, it seems that the parents usually seem to favour their sons. ^754

143. Could Rinpoche please explain the detailed process for helping the dead? What should we do or what can be done when a friend or relative of ours is dying, both when he or she is Buddhist and when that person is not Buddhist? ^755

It depends on what kind of people they are. Even if they are Buddhists, some people just call themselves Buddhists, or were born as Buddhists, or are intellectually Buddhists but never practiced. But if someone is Buddhist and has practiced a little bit, then it makes a big difference. As well, some individuals who are not Buddhists, if they are calm, have a compassionate nature, have been very generous, and have contentment in their lives, then probably death will be much less panicky. ^756

It’s important to keep in mind that one of the biggest reasons why we are afraid of death is because there is uncertainty. This is one place you have never been in this life, and even though a lot of people have died, they never come back to tell us how it is. ^757

So this is one territory with which we are not at all familiar, and it is also one territory that is totally different from what we have now. What we have right now ⎯ a postal address, WeChat address, email account, bank account, telephone number, citizenship, a reference, a direction ⎯ all that is going to stop. You can take with you nothing that will remind you who you are. ^758

There are a lot of people who may not believe in reincarnation or what happens after death. Then of course, that's the end. If you strongly believe that death is like the exhaustion of fire or the evaporation of water, then my answer is useless. But if you do think body and mind in principle are separate things, then when body and mind separate, which is what we call death, the body gets buried or cremated or eaten by birds, but the mind is a separate entity that continues without its life-long companion, which is the body. ^759

Until now, mind has never known how to function without the body. If you are a meditator, probably you do know. But for most of us, the moment we look at something, even though the mind is the one that is looking, it’s the eyes you are using. It’s the same with the nose, the skin, and all our senses ⎯ you always use this physical body as the interpreter. Actually, that interpretation has even become more gross, because we are not only depending on this body as an interpreter, but have even more complicated interpreters like education, the political system, values, magazines and so on. All these interpreters are going to stop at death. ^760

At the time of death, the mind suddenly becomes so naked; and for many people who have not been working with their minds, it may be quite an unexpected problem. Imagine you have never been to Istanbul by yourself, and you have lost everything ⎯ your passport, your telephone book, and you don't remember the telephone numbers of any of your friends and family. You have not one penny, you don't speak their language, and in fact you look strange to them, which creates panic in your heart, and then you begin to act strangely. Then the Istanbul people begin to think you are crazy and they try to catch you and put you in some sort of confined area like a prison. ^761

At least if you are in Istanbul, there is still a reference ⎯ you are a human being, and they are human beings. But when you die, when body and mind are separated, even the notion and reference of human being slowly or abruptly vanishes. That referencelessness is the most scary for people. But for the yogis and practitioners, this is what they have always been trying to reach⎯ it is an opportunity to grab. ^762

Anyway, depending on what kind of people you are dealing with, if the person already has a good foundation of dharma practice, then all you need to do is go near this person who is dying, and remind him his practice, such as the view. ^763

I had an incredible experience when KyabjeDezhung Rinpoche was passing away and requested Dilgo Kyentse Rinpoche to come and visit him. Both of them are my gurus. I was attending Dilgo Kyentse Rinpoche. Dezhung Rinpoche was in his last remaining hours, and it was incredible to witness how Dilgo Kyentse Rinpoche and Dezhung Rinpoche had a casual conversation on the absolute view. And we had tea, and that was it. We left Dezhung Rinpoche, and in few hours we received a call that he had just passed. ^764

Now even if you are not that well prepared but have a little knowledge of Buddhism, especially in Vajrayana, there is a text such as the Bardo Todhol, which actually contains all the information about the experiences during the bardo stage, and how to handle those experiences. It is called the Tibetan Book of the Dead. ^765

Now for ordinary people who still have some sort of affinity for the Buddhadharma and a sort of adoration or devotion towards the Buddha, we can recite the names of the great Buddhas and bodhisattvas, such as Avalokitesvara. ^766

If the person is not a Buddhist at all, and in fact maybe even anti-Buddhist, then the best thing you can do is make him relaxed, and   without this person knowing that you are doing prayers, you should do prayers. I am saying this because there is no reason to make him more agitated. Then there are other things you can do, such as let the dying person ⎯ even the dead person ⎯ have chakras[76] and mandalas next to their bodies. These mantras have so much power. ^767

If you happen to know the ritual ⎯ or even if you don’t know it, there are people like monks who do know ⎯ then it is good to recite sutras, burn food, particularly during the usual mealtimes, like lunch, dinner and breakfast, and dedicate the smell of the food to the dying person, especially towards the beginning of the dying process, while chanting the Avalokitesvara Mantra (Om Mani Padme Hum)[77] with compassion. ^768

It is believed that bardo beings roam around in the bardo stage for 49 days, but that is a very big generalization. It can be as short as a moment or several days, and as long as 49 years or eons. If the remaining relatives and friends can do good deeds such as making lamp offerings, incense offerings and flower offerings, and reading sutras such as pure land sutras like the Amitabha Sutra, it is certainly going to be a big help. ^769
